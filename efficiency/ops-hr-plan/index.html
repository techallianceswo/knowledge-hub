    Ops & HR plan

    Build the capacity to deliver today and grow tomorrow

    You've tested your solution, started developing your sales process, and identified how you want to reach more customers.

    Now you need to make sure your business can deliver what it sells.

    An operations and human resources (Ops & HR) plan describes how your company will organize its people, processes, tools, and other resources to deliver its products or services.

    It also identifies what will need to change as your business grows.

    At this stage, you don't need a complex organizational structure or a detailed operations manual covering every activity.

    You need a practical plan that answers two questions:

    1. Can we reliably deliver our current offering at the service level we've promised?
    2. What additional people, resources, and investments will we need as demand increases?

    Your plan should help you:

    • Understand how your business delivers value to customers
    • Define responsibilities and essential workflows
    • Estimate how many customers you can currently serve
    • Identify operational bottlenecks and risks
    • Establish basic people management practices
    • Plan future hiring, equipment, software, and infrastructure needs
    • Estimate the costs and timing of growth
    • Prepare reliable assumptions for cash flow forecasting and funding analysis

    The goal isn't to build a large organization before you need one. It's to understand what your business needs today and what it will take to support the next stage of growth.

    Define the service level you want to deliver

    Start by defining what customers should consistently expect from your business.

    Your service level describes the quality, availability, responsiveness, and reliability of the product or service you intend to provide.

    For example, depending on your offering, customers may expect:

    • Delivery within a specific timeframe
    • Responses to support requests within one business day
    • Reliable access to a software platform
    • A consistent onboarding experience
    • Regular progress updates
    • Products that meet defined quality requirements
    • Access to a particular level of expertise

    You don't need to promise the fastest response or highest level of service in your industry.

    You need to establish expectations that matter to customers and that your business can realistically meet.

    Review your customer discovery findings, solution interviews, pilot results, and sales commitments.

    Ask yourself:

    • What do customers need from us to receive the intended value?
    • What have we promised during sales conversations?
    • What level of service can we currently deliver?
    • Which activities are essential to maintaining quality?
    • What would happen if customer demand increased?

    Be specific where possible.

    For example, "provide excellent customer support" is difficult to plan around.

    "Respond to customer support requests within one business day" gives you a clearer operational requirement.

    Avoid making commitments that your current resources cannot support.

    Map how your business delivers value

    Once you've defined your service level, identify the activities required to deliver it.

    Think about what happens after a customer agrees to purchase your offering.

    Depending on your business, the process might include:

    1. Receiving and confirming the order or agreement
    2. Collecting customer requirements
    3. Preparing or configuring the product or service
    4. Delivering the offering
    5. Providing onboarding or training
    6. Responding to customer questions
    7. Monitoring quality and resolving problems
    8. Managing ongoing service or renewals

    For a software company, the process may involve account setup, hosting, product maintenance, security, and customer support.

    For a consulting business, it may involve project scoping, assigning team members, delivering services, and reviewing results.

    For a physical product business, it may involve purchasing materials, manufacturing, inventory, quality control, and shipping.

    Map the activities that matter most to your business.

    For each activity, identify:

    • Who is responsible
    • What resources are required
    • How much time it takes
    • What tools or systems are involved
    • What could delay or interrupt delivery
    • How you'll know the activity was completed successfully

    You don't need to document every minor task.

    Focus on the workflows that directly affect your ability to serve customers.

    Understand your current operating capacity

    Before planning growth, establish how much your business can deliver with its current resources.

    Operating capacity is the amount of work your business can complete within a given period while maintaining its intended service level.

    Your capacity may depend on:

    • Available working hours
    • Employee or founder expertise
    • Equipment and production capabilities
    • Software or infrastructure limits
    • Supplier availability
    • Physical space
    • Customer support requirements
    • Time spent on administration and management

    For example, a consulting startup might estimate that each client requires 20 hours of delivery work per month.

    If the team has 100 hours available for client delivery, its theoretical capacity would be five clients.

    However, the team may need to reserve time for unexpected issues, internal meetings, training, and other responsibilities.

    Your practical capacity may therefore be lower.

    Similarly, a manufacturing business might have equipment capable of producing 500 units per month but only enough labour or materials to deliver 300.

    The lowest relevant constraint determines its current capacity.

    Identify your bottlenecks

    A bottleneck is an activity or resource that limits how much work your business can complete.

    Common examples include:

    • One founder approving every customer deliverable
    • Limited manufacturing equipment
    • A shortage of qualified technical staff
    • Manual onboarding that takes too much time
    • Dependence on one supplier
    • Customer support handled by someone who also manages sales

    Identifying bottlenecks helps you decide where additional resources or process improvements would make the biggest difference.

    Don't assume hiring is always the answer.

    Sometimes a clearer process, better software, improved scheduling, or a reliable external provider can increase capacity without adding a permanent employee.

    Define roles and responsibilities

    Even a small startup benefits from clear responsibilities.

    Start by listing the functions required to operate your business.

    These might include:

    • Leadership and business planning
    • Product development
    • Service delivery or production
    • Sales and marketing
    • Customer onboarding and support
    • Finance and administration
    • People management
    • Technology and information security
    • Legal and compliance coordination

    You don't need a separate employee for every function.

    In an early-stage startup, one founder may handle several responsibilities.

    The important thing is to identify who is accountable for each activity.

    For example:

    Function Current owner Additional support needed?
    Product development Founder A
    Sales Founder B
    Customer onboarding Founder A
    Customer support Founder A
    Bookkeeping External provider
    HR administration Founder B

    Look for responsibilities that are unclear, duplicated, or concentrated in one person.

    Consider what would happen if that person became unavailable.

    A simple organizational chart or responsibility table is often enough at this stage.

    Establish essential people management practices

    Human resources planning isn't only about deciding when to hire.

    It's also about creating an environment where people understand their responsibilities, have the tools to perform their work, and receive appropriate support.

    If you already have employees, consider whether you've established basic practices for:

    • Employment agreements and role expectations
    • Compensation and payroll
    • Working hours, time off, and applicable employment standards
    • Workplace health and safety
    • Employee onboarding
    • Training and professional development
    • Performance feedback
    • Communication and collaboration
    • Confidentiality and information security
    • Employee records and privacy

    The requirements will depend on your location, industry, workforce, and employment arrangements.

    In Ontario, for example, relevant employment standards and workplace obligations may apply even to small businesses.

    Consult appropriate government resources or qualified professionals when establishing employment policies, compensation arrangements, or contracts.

    Prepare for your first hires

    If you're not employing anyone yet, you don't need to create a full HR department.

    However, you should understand what will be required before hiring your first employee.

    Consider:

    • What role is needed?
    • What responsibilities will the person have?
    • What skills and experience are essential?
    • Who will supervise and support them?
    • What compensation can the business afford?
    • What payroll, insurance, and employment obligations apply?
    • What equipment, software, and training will they need?
    • How long will it take them to become productive?

    Remember that an employee's cost is more than their salary.

    Your financial plan may need to account for employer payroll contributions, benefits, recruiting, equipment, software, training, and other employment-related expenses.

    Identify the resources required to operate

    People are only one part of your operating capacity.

    Review the other resources your business needs to deliver its offering.

    Software and technology

    Identify the systems required for product delivery, collaboration, customer management, security, and administration.

    For each system, consider:

    • What is it used for?
    • How much does it cost?
    • Is the cost fixed or usage-based?
    • Will the cost increase with more customers or employees?
    • Are there limits that could affect growth?

    Equipment and infrastructure

    Consider computers, production equipment, tools, vehicles, workspace, and other physical resources.

    Determine what you already have, what needs maintenance or replacement, and what additional equipment may be required.

    Suppliers and external providers

    Identify external organizations your business depends on.

    These might include manufacturers, distributors, cloud providers, contractors, accountants, or specialized service providers.

    Consider their capacity, pricing, lead times, reliability, and the consequences of interruptions.

    Operating expenses

    Document the recurring costs required to keep the business running.

    Examples include:

    • Salaries and employer-related costs
    • Contractor fees
    • Software subscriptions
    • Rent and utilities
    • Hosting and infrastructure
    • Insurance
    • Equipment maintenance
    • Professional services
    • Materials and supplies
    • Shipping and logistics

    Distinguish between costs that remain relatively stable and costs that increase as your business serves more customers.

    This distinction will be important for your cash flow forecast.

    Build a growth plan based on capacity

    Once you understand your current operations, plan what needs to change as demand increases.

    Avoid assuming that your business will grow at a constant rate or that every additional customer requires the same resources.

    Some businesses can serve many more customers with relatively small increases in cost.

    Others need additional staff, equipment, or infrastructure at specific points.

    For example, a software business may be able to support more users without immediately hiring additional employees, but eventually need more customer support or infrastructure.

    A service business may need additional delivery staff much sooner because its capacity depends directly on available working hours.

    Define growth scenarios

    Consider at least three operating scenarios:

    Current operations

    What does the business need to deliver its existing commitments?

    Near-term growth

    What would you need if sales increase over the next several months?

    Higher-growth scenario

    What additional investments would be necessary if demand grows faster than expected?

    For each scenario, estimate:

    • Customers or units served
    • Workload and service requirements
    • Staffing needs
    • Equipment and software requirements
    • External provider costs
    • One-time investments
    • Recurring operating expenses
    • Capacity limits and potential bottlenecks

    Use your sales goals and pipeline assumptions to inform these scenarios.

    Don't assume that your most optimistic sales target will automatically happen.

    Plan when to hire or invest

    A growth plan should explain not only what resources you'll need, but when you'll need them.

    For example, you might decide to hire a customer support specialist when the number of active customers reaches a level that the founding team can no longer support reliably.

    Or you might need additional production equipment before accepting orders beyond your current manufacturing capacity.

    These are capacity triggers: measurable conditions that indicate when a change is needed.

    Examples include:

    • Active customer volume
    • Number of projects in progress
    • Production volume
    • Average support response time
    • Staff workload
    • Equipment utilization
    • Delivery backlog
    • Recurring revenue, where it supports the financial commitment

    Try to plan ahead of the point where capacity becomes a problem.

    Hiring, purchasing equipment, and implementing new systems take time.

    For example, if recruiting and onboarding a new employee takes two months, waiting until your team is already overwhelmed may affect customer service.

    Include realistic preparation and implementation time in your growth plan.

    Connect your Ops & HR plan to financial planning

    Your operations plan should become a practical input for the cash flow and funding analysis you'll complete later.

    The goal is to translate your operating decisions into financial assumptions.

    For each planned resource, identify:

    • What: The employee, equipment, software, service, or other resource required
    • Why: The operational need it addresses
    • When: The expected start or purchase date
    • Cost: The estimated one-time and recurring expenses
    • Trigger: The customer demand or operational condition that makes it necessary
    • Lead time: How long it takes to recruit, purchase, implement, or prepare
    • Confidence: Whether the estimate is confirmed, researched, or preliminary

    For example:

    Resource Trigger Timing One-time cost Recurring cost
    Customer support employee Customer volume exceeds team capacity Month 7 Recruiting and equipment Salary and employer costs
    Additional software licences Team grows to five people Month 5 Setup, if applicable Monthly subscriptions
    Production equipment Forecast orders exceed current capacity Month 10 Equipment purchase Maintenance and operating costs

    These are illustrative scenarios, not recommended hiring thresholds.

    Your actual plan should use assumptions relevant to your business.

    Consider the timing of cash outflows

    Some investments need to happen before the additional revenue arrives.

    For example, you may need to:

    • Hire and train employees before they can serve customers
    • Purchase inventory before receiving customer payments
    • Buy equipment before increasing production
    • Pay software or infrastructure costs before launching a service
    • Cover operating expenses while waiting for customers to pay invoices

    This timing matters.

    A business can have promising sales opportunities and still face cash flow challenges if it needs to spend money before collecting revenue.

    Your Ops & HR plan should help identify these funding requirements before they become urgent.

    Review and adjust your plan

    Your operating plan isn't a fixed commitment to hire or spend according to a predetermined schedule.

    It's a working plan that should evolve as your business learns more about demand, delivery costs, and team capacity.

    Review it periodically alongside your sales results.

    Ask:

    • Are we delivering the service level we promised?
    • Is our team operating at a sustainable workload?
    • Are any bottlenecks becoming more serious?
    • Have our costs changed?
    • Are our growth assumptions still realistic?
    • Do we need to hire, automate, outsource, or change a process?
    • Should any planned investments be delayed or brought forward?

    Update the plan when your assumptions change.

    Your operations should support your business goals, not create unnecessary costs before the demand exists.

    RACI matrix

    A RACI matrix helps clarify responsibilities for important activities.

    RACI stands for:

    • Responsible: The person completing the work
    • Accountable: The person ultimately answerable for the result
    • Consulted: People whose input is needed
    • Informed: People who need updates

    You don't need to use RACI for every small task.

    It can be particularly useful when multiple founders, employees, or contractors are involved in a process.

    Simple operating procedure template

    Document the most important recurring activities using a short standard operating procedure (SOP).

    For each process, include:

    Process name: What activity does this document cover?

    Purpose: Why does the process matter?

    Owner: Who is responsible?

    Trigger: When does the process begin?

    Steps: What needs to happen, and in what order?

    Tools and resources: What is required?

    Quality check: How do you know the work was completed correctly?

    Exceptions: What should happen when something goes wrong?

    Start with processes that affect customer delivery, quality, safety, or business continuity.

    Avoid creating documentation that your team won't use.

    Tech support

    Google Sheets

    Google Sheets can help you estimate operating capacity, track staffing needs, compare growth scenarios, and organize cost assumptions.

    It is a practical starting point for building an Ops & HR plan that can later be connected to financial forecasts.

    Microsoft Excel

    Microsoft Excel can help you build more detailed capacity and resource models, including scenarios that compare staffing, equipment, and operating costs at different levels of demand.

    Trello

    Trello can help you organize recurring operational tasks, assign responsibilities, and track work through simple workflows.

    It may be useful for small teams that need more structure without adopting a complex operations system.

    Notion

    Notion can help you maintain an internal operations workspace containing responsibilities, SOPs, onboarding materials, and team documentation.

    Start with the information your team needs most rather than building an extensive knowledge base immediately.

    Miro

    Miro can help you visualize workflows, map responsibilities, identify bottlenecks, and plan how your organizational structure might change as the business grows.

    Put it into practice

    Create a practical Ops & HR plan that supports your current customers and prepares your business for growth.

    By the end of this exercise, you should know what your business needs to operate today, how much it can currently deliver, and what additional resources and costs may be required as demand increases.

    Start by reviewing your sales goals, current customer commitments, and findings from your MVP or pilot.

    Then:

    1. Define your service level. Describe what customers should consistently expect from your business.
    2. Map your operations. Identify the essential activities required to deliver your product or service.
    3. Assign responsibilities. Document who currently manages each important activity.
    4. Estimate your capacity. Determine how many customers, projects, or units your existing resources can support.
    5. Identify bottlenecks. Find the activities or resources most likely to limit delivery or affect quality.
    6. Review your people practices. Confirm that essential employment, onboarding, communication, and team support needs are addressed.
    7. Inventory your resources. List the people, software, equipment, infrastructure, and external providers required to operate.
    8. Build growth scenarios. Estimate what your business would need at different levels of customer demand.
    9. Plan future hires and investments. Identify the resources you'll need, the conditions that trigger them, and how long they'll take to put in place.
    10. Estimate costs. Record one-time investments, recurring expenses, expected timing, and the assumptions behind your figures.
    11. Document your plan. Bring the information together in a short, practical document or spreadsheet.
    12. Review it regularly. Update your capacity, staffing, and cost assumptions as sales and operational needs change.

    Before moving forward, ask yourself:

    • Can we consistently deliver the service level we've promised?
    • Do we understand the activities required to serve each customer?
    • Is someone responsible for every essential function?
    • How much additional demand can we handle with our current resources?
    • What is our most important operational bottleneck?
    • Are we relying too heavily on one person, supplier, or system?
    • Do we have the basic people management practices our team needs?
    • What would need to change if our customer base doubled?
    • When would we need to hire or invest in additional resources?
    • Have we considered the full cost of employees, equipment, software, and external services?
    • Do we know which expenses are one-time and which are recurring?
    • Have we documented when those expenses are likely to occur?

    Consider reviewing your plan with your TechAlliance advisor, particularly if you're preparing to hire, expand operations, or seek funding.

    Prepare for cash flow and funding analysis

    Your final Ops & HR plan should include a resource forecast that can be transferred into your financial planning.

    At minimum, prepare:

    • Current operating costs: What you spend today to run the business.
    • Planned hires: Roles, expected start dates, and estimated total employment costs.
    • Planned investments: Equipment, infrastructure, software, and other purchases.
    • Growth-related expenses: Costs that increase as customer demand grows.
    • Timing assumptions: When expenses will occur and how far in advance you need to commit.
    • Capacity assumptions: How many customers or units each operating scenario can support.

    Keep your estimates realistic and identify where additional research or quotes are needed.

    Remember: The purpose of your Ops & HR plan isn't to predict every future operational decision.

    It's to make sure you can deliver what you've promised today, recognize what growth will require tomorrow, and understand the financial implications before making major commitments.

    A clear plan will help you build a more reliable cash flow forecast and make better decisions about when, why, and how much funding your business may need.
