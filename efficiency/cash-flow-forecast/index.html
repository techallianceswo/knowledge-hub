    Cash flow forecast

    Know when money comes in, when it goes out, and how long it will last

    You've developed your sales process, defined your operating needs, and started planning the resources required to grow your business.

    Now it's time to understand what those plans mean for your cash.

    A cash flow forecast estimates how much money your business expects to receive and spend over a defined period.

    It helps you understand whether you'll have enough cash to pay your bills, support your operations, and make planned investments.

    A cash flow forecast can help you answer questions such as:

    • How much cash do we have available today?
    • When do we expect customers to pay us?
    • How much will it cost to operate each month?
    • Can we afford our planned hires and investments?
    • What happens if sales are lower than expected?
    • When might we run short of cash?
    • How much additional funding might we need, and when?

    A business can generate revenue and still run out of cash.

    For example, imagine your company signs a $20,000 contract in January, but the customer won't pay until March.

    Meanwhile, you need to pay employees, software subscriptions, and suppliers in January and February.

    Even though you've made a sale, you still need enough cash to cover those expenses before the payment arrives.

    Your cash flow forecast helps you see these gaps before they become problems.

    Understand cash flow, revenue, and profit

    These terms are connected, but they mean different things.

    Revenue is the income your business earns from selling products or services.

    Profit is what remains after subtracting the expenses associated with generating that revenue, according to the accounting method used.

    Cash flow tracks the actual movement of money into and out of your business.

    For example:

    You invoice a customer for $5,000 in January.

    The customer pays in March.

    Depending on your accounting method, you may recognize the revenue before March. However, the cash doesn't enter your bank account until the customer pays.

    Similarly, you might purchase equipment in one month even though its cost is recognized over a longer period in your financial statements.

    Your cash flow forecast focuses on when money is actually received or paid.

    It isn't a replacement for bookkeeping, an income statement, or other financial reports.

    It's a planning tool that helps you understand your future cash position.

    Start with the information you've already collected

    You don't need to begin your forecast from scratch.

    Much of the information should come from the work you've completed throughout the Knowledge Hub.

    Sales assets and process

    Your sales planning provides assumptions about:

    • Expected customer acquisition
    • Average sale value
    • Sales targets
    • Sales cycle length
    • Pricing
    • Customer payment terms
    • Recurring or repeat purchases

    Use these assumptions to estimate future customer payments.

    Remember that a sales target isn't guaranteed revenue, and expected revenue isn't necessarily cash collected in the same month.

    Ops & HR plan

    Your operations plan provides information about:

    • Current operating expenses
    • Salaries and employer-related costs
    • Contractor fees
    • Software and infrastructure
    • Equipment purchases
    • Hiring plans
    • Capacity limits
    • Growth-related investments

    Use these estimates to forecast when your business will need to spend money.

    Pay particular attention to costs that must be paid before additional sales can be delivered.

    MVP and pilot results

    Your Validation-stage work may provide early evidence about:

    • What customers are willing to pay
    • How often they purchase or use your offering
    • The cost of delivering the product or service
    • How long onboarding takes
    • Customer support requirements
    • Whether customers continue after an initial purchase

    Use actual results where available.

    If you're still making assumptions, document them clearly.

    Choose a practical forecasting period

    For most early-stage startups, a 12-month forecast organized by month is a useful starting point.

    It provides enough visibility to plan operating expenses, customer payments, and upcoming investments without requiring an overly complex model.

    If you're preparing a funding application or planning major investments, you may need to forecast 24 months or longer.

    Some businesses also benefit from a short-term weekly forecast, particularly when cash is tight or large payments are approaching.

    For example:

    • Weekly forecast: Helps manage immediate payment obligations and short-term cash availability.
    • Monthly forecast: Helps plan operating costs, hiring, investments, and funding needs.
    • Longer-term forecast: Helps evaluate growth scenarios and future capital requirements.

    Start with the level of detail your business needs.

    A simple forecast that you regularly update is more useful than a complicated model that becomes outdated.

    Estimate your cash inflows

    Cash inflows are the amounts of money you expect to receive.

    Depending on your business, they may include:

    • Customer payments
    • Subscription payments
    • Deposits and advance payments
    • Grants or contributions
    • Loans
    • Equity investments
    • Other cash receipts

    Separate cash generated by business operations from money received through financing.

    This helps you understand whether the business can support itself through customer revenue or currently depends on outside capital.

    Estimate customer payments

    Start with your sales forecast.

    For each product or service, consider:

    • How many sales do you expect?
    • What will customers pay?
    • When will the sale occur?
    • When will the customer actually pay?
    • Will the payment be received all at once or in instalments?

    For example:

    Month Expected sales Expected cash collected
    January $10,000 $4,000
    February $15,000 $9,000
    March $20,000 $17,000

    These figures are illustrative.

    The difference between sales and collections could result from payment terms, deposits, or invoices that haven't been paid yet.

    Use your actual payment terms and customer behaviour wherever possible.

    Be careful with uncertain funding

    If you're applying for a grant, loan, or investment, don't automatically treat the funding as guaranteed.

    Record the expected amount, potential timing, and current status.

    Consider preparing a scenario that excludes funding you haven't secured.

    This will help you understand whether your business can continue operating if the money arrives later than expected or doesn't arrive.

    Estimate your cash outflows

    Cash outflows are the payments your business expects to make.

    Start with your Ops & HR plan and list the expenses required to operate.

    Fixed and recurring expenses

    These may include:

    • Salaries and payroll-related costs
    • Rent
    • Insurance
    • Software subscriptions
    • Internet and communications
    • Accounting and professional services
    • Regular loan payments

    Some expenses may be paid monthly, while others are quarterly or annually.

    Record them in the months when payment is expected.

    Variable expenses

    These change with your sales volume or level of activity.

    Examples include:

    • Materials and inventory
    • Manufacturing
    • Shipping
    • Payment processing fees
    • Usage-based software or hosting
    • Contractors paid per project
    • Sales commissions

    Use your expected sales and delivery volume to estimate these costs.

    For example, if each product costs $40 to manufacture and you expect to produce 100 units, your estimated manufacturing cost would be $4,000.

    However, you also need to consider when you'll pay for the materials or production.

    One-time investments

    These may include:

    • Equipment purchases
    • Website or software development
    • Office setup
    • Initial inventory
    • Professional services
    • Recruitment and onboarding expenses
    • New infrastructure

    Use the timing established in your Ops & HR growth plan.

    Taxes and other obligations

    Consider applicable tax payments, payroll remittances, loan repayments, and other financial obligations.

    For Canadian businesses, GST/HST collection and remittance may affect cash timing.

    Amounts collected on behalf of tax authorities shouldn't be treated as unrestricted operating cash.

    Tax treatment and remittance requirements depend on your circumstances. Consult a qualified accountant or relevant government guidance when preparing these estimates.

    Build your monthly cash flow forecast

    Once you've estimated inflows and outflows, organize them into a spreadsheet.

    A basic forecast follows this structure:

    Opening cash balance + Cash inflows - Cash outflows = Closing cash balance

    The closing balance from one month becomes the opening balance for the next.

    For example:

    Category January February March
    Opening cash balance $25,000 $21,000 $17,000
    Customer payments $8,000 $12,000 $16,000
    Other cash inflows $0 $0 $0
    Total cash inflows $8,000 $12,000 $16,000
    Operating payments $10,000 $13,000 $14,000
    Equipment purchases $2,000 $3,000 $0
    Total cash outflows $12,000 $16,000 $14,000
    Net cash flow -$4,000 -$4,000 $2,000
    Closing cash balance $21,000 $17,000 $19,000

    In this example, the business spends more than it receives in January and February.

    Its cash balance begins to recover in March.

    However, the business still needs to understand whether its available cash will be sufficient to support future expenses and growth.

    Check the lowest projected cash balance

    Don't focus only on your cash position at the end of the year.

    Look at each month to identify when cash is expected to be lowest.

    For businesses with significant payments occurring within a month, a monthly forecast may hide short-term shortages.

    If necessary, create a weekly forecast for periods when cash availability is particularly important.

    Understand your burn rate and runway

    Two useful financial measures for startups are burn rate and cash runway.

    Burn rate

    Burn rate describes how quickly your business is using cash.

    Gross burn generally refers to total cash operating outflows during a period.

    Net burn refers to the amount of cash your business consumes after accounting for operating cash inflows.

    For example:

    If your business spends $15,000 per month and receives $8,000 from customers, its net operating cash burn is $7,000 per month.

    If operating cash inflows exceed operating outflows, the business may be generating cash rather than burning it.

    Be consistent about which cash flows you include in your calculations.

    Cash runway

    Cash runway estimates how long your available cash can support your business if it continues consuming cash.

    A simple estimate is:

    Cash runway = Available cash ÷ Average monthly net burn

    For example:

    If you have $42,000 in available cash and your net burn is $7,000 per month, your estimated runway is six months.

    However, this calculation assumes your burn rate remains constant.

    In reality, customer payments, hiring, equipment purchases, and other expenses may change significantly.

    Use your month-by-month forecast to identify when cash could run out rather than relying only on a simple runway calculation.

    Prepare different financial scenarios

    Your forecast is based on assumptions about future events.

    Some of those assumptions will be wrong.

    Instead of relying on a single projection, prepare a few scenarios to understand how different outcomes could affect your business.

    Base scenario

    Your most reasonable estimate based on current evidence and operating plans.

    It might assume:

    • Sales develop according to your current forecast.
    • Customers pay according to expected terms.
    • Planned expenses occur on schedule.
    • Hiring follows your Ops & HR plan.

    Conservative scenario

    A scenario where important assumptions don't work out as expected.

    For example:

    • Sales are lower than anticipated.
    • Customers take longer to pay.
    • A major contract is delayed.
    • Operating expenses increase.
    • Planned funding arrives later than expected.

    This scenario helps you identify risks and prepare responses before cash becomes tight.

    Growth scenario

    A scenario where demand increases faster than expected.

    For example:

    • More customers purchase your offering.
    • Revenue grows faster than planned.
    • You need additional employees sooner.
    • Production or infrastructure costs increase.
    • You must invest before receiving all the additional customer payments.

    Faster growth doesn't always improve your immediate cash position.

    It may create additional working capital needs.

    Compare the results

    For each scenario, review:

    • Lowest projected cash balance
    • Months with negative net cash flow
    • Expected runway
    • Hiring and investment requirements
    • Potential funding gaps
    • Decisions you may need to make

    Don't choose arbitrary growth percentages and treat them as reliable forecasts.

    Use actual sales evidence, operating constraints, and reasonable assumptions.

    Identify potential funding gaps

    One of the most important outcomes of your forecast is understanding when your business might need additional capital.

    A funding gap occurs when the cash available to your business is insufficient to cover planned payments and maintain an appropriate operating buffer.

    For example, your forecast might show that:

    • You have enough cash to operate for the next four months.
    • A planned equipment purchase will reduce your cash balance significantly.
    • Customer payments aren't expected to increase until several months later.
    • You may need additional funding before making that investment.

    This information gives you time to evaluate your options.

    Depending on your circumstances, those options might include:

    • Adjusting the timing of an investment
    • Reducing or postponing discretionary expenses
    • Improving customer payment collection
    • Revisiting payment terms
    • Changing your growth plan
    • Exploring financing or investment opportunities

    Avoid assuming that outside funding will arrive exactly when needed.

    Loans, grants, and investments can involve application processes, approvals, negotiations, and delays.

    Your forecast should help you identify when to begin exploring funding, not just when your bank balance is approaching zero.

    Review actual results and update your forecast

    Your first cash flow forecast won't be perfectly accurate.

    That's expected.

    Its value comes from using it regularly to understand your business and improve your decisions.

    At the end of each month:

    1. Record the cash you actually received.
    2. Record the payments you actually made.
    3. Compare the results with your forecast.
    4. Identify the largest differences.
    5. Investigate why those differences occurred.
    6. Update your assumptions for the months ahead.

    For example, if customers consistently take 45 days to pay instead of the 30 days you expected, adjust your future cash collection assumptions.

    If your hosting costs increase faster than expected, update your operating expense forecast.

    If you delay a planned hire, adjust the timing of the related costs.

    Distinguish timing differences from ongoing changes

    Not every difference between forecast and actual results indicates a problem with your business model.

    A payment arriving one month late may be a timing difference.

    A recurring pattern of customers paying later than expected may require a change to your assumptions or collection process.

    Similarly, a one-time equipment purchase is different from an ongoing increase in monthly operating costs.

    Understanding the cause helps you make better decisions.

    Your forecast should evolve as you learn more about how your business actually operates.

    Monthly cash flow forecast

    Use a spreadsheet with one column for each month.

    Organize it into five main sections:

    1. Opening cash balance

    The cash available at the beginning of the month.

    2. Cash inflows

    Customer payments, financing received, and other cash receipts.

    3. Cash outflows

    Operating expenses, payroll, supplier payments, equipment purchases, taxes, debt payments, and other cash payments.

    4. Net cash flow

    Total cash inflows minus total cash outflows.

    5. Closing cash balance

    Opening cash balance plus net cash flow.

    Extend the forecast for at least 12 months, or longer if required for your planning or funding application.

    Google Sheets can help you create and maintain a monthly cash flow forecast, collaborate with team members, and update assumptions as your business changes.

    A well-organized spreadsheet is often sufficient for an early-stage startup.

    Use accounting records to improve the accuracy of your forecast.

    It may be useful for maintaining the financial records that inform your cash flow projections.

    Put it into practice

    Create a cash flow forecast that shows how your business expects to receive and spend money over the coming months.

    By the end of this exercise, you should understand your projected cash position, the assumptions behind it, and when you may need additional funding or changes to your operating plan.

    Start by gathering your sales projections, Ops & HR plan, current financial records, and planned investments.

    Then:

    1. Choose your forecasting period. Start with 12 months of monthly projections, or use a longer period if required for funding or business planning.
    2. Record your opening cash balance. Use the actual cash available to the business at the beginning of the forecast.
    3. Estimate customer payments. Use your sales plan, pricing, expected sales volume, and payment terms to project when cash will be collected.
    4. Identify other inflows. Include grants, loans, investments, and other expected receipts, clearly distinguishing confirmed funding from uncertain possibilities.
    5. List recurring expenses. Include payroll, software, rent, professional services, and other operating payments.
    6. Estimate variable expenses. Calculate the costs that change with sales or delivery volume.
    7. Add planned investments. Use your Ops & HR plan to include hiring costs, equipment purchases, and other growth-related expenses in the appropriate months.
    8. Calculate monthly cash balances. Determine total inflows, total outflows, net cash flow, and closing cash for each month.
    9. Identify your lowest cash position. Look for months when cash may become insufficient to support operations.
    10. Build alternative scenarios. Compare your base forecast with conservative and growth scenarios.
    11. Identify potential funding needs. Determine whether additional capital may be required, how much, and when.
    12. Set a review schedule. Update the forecast monthly using actual results and revised assumptions.

    Before moving forward, ask yourself:

    • Do I understand the difference between sales, profit, and cash flow?
    • Have I based my forecast on the sales and operating plans I've developed?
    • Have I considered when customers will actually pay?
    • Have I included payroll, taxes, software, suppliers, and other recurring obligations?
    • Have I included the one-time investments needed for growth?
    • Have I accounted for costs that increase as sales grow?
    • Have I clearly identified assumptions and uncertain funding?
    • Do I know when my cash balance is expected to be lowest?
    • What happens if sales are delayed or expenses increase?
    • Can I afford my planned hiring and investments?
    • Do I know when I may need additional capital?
    • Have I established a process for updating my forecast?

    Consider reviewing your forecast with your TechAlliance advisor and, where appropriate, an accountant or financial professional.

    Prepare for funding analysis

    Your cash flow forecast should help you identify:

    • How much funding may be needed: The projected shortfall plus an appropriate operating buffer.
    • When funding may be needed: The period before your cash position becomes insufficient.
    • What the funding would support: Operating expenses, hiring, equipment, inventory, product development, or other business needs.
    • How long the funding could last: The expected runway under different scenarios.
    • What could change the requirement: Sales performance, payment timing, cost reductions, or changes to your growth plan.

    Keep these findings available for your upcoming funding analysis.

    Remember: A cash flow forecast isn't a prediction you need to get exactly right.

    It's a tool for understanding your business, testing your assumptions, and making informed financial decisions.

    The more consistently you update it, the more useful it becomes.
