# Product roadmap

## Turn customer insights into a clear direction for your product

You've spoken with potential customers, explored the problems they experience, and gathered insights from subject-matter experts.

Now it's time to organize what you've learned and decide what your product should focus on.

A **product roadmap** is a visual plan that communicates the direction of your product, the problems you want to address, and the priorities that will guide its development.

It helps you connect your research to the decisions you'll make as you explore, design, test, and eventually build your solution.

At the Discovery stage, your roadmap doesn't need to be a detailed development schedule or a list of features with fixed delivery dates.

Instead, it should help you answer three questions:

- **What problems are we trying to solve?**
- **Which problems should we focus on first?**
- **What do we still need to learn before deciding how to solve them?**

Your initial roadmap is a working plan. It should evolve as you gather more evidence and develop a better understanding of your customers.

## Start with what you've learned

Your customer discovery interviews and subject-matter expert conversations should be the starting point for your roadmap.

Review your interview notes and identify recurring themes.

For example, potential customers might have described:

- Tasks that take too much time
- Processes that are difficult or confusing
- Existing tools that don't meet their needs
- Expensive or inefficient workarounds
- Problems that affect their ability to complete important work

Subject-matter experts may have also identified technical limitations, industry requirements, or other considerations that could influence your approach.

Organize these findings into a list of customer problems, needs, and opportunities.

**Focus on the underlying problem rather than immediately deciding which feature to build.**

For example, a customer might say they need an automated reporting dashboard.

Before adding a dashboard to your roadmap, investigate what they're actually trying to accomplish.

Perhaps their real challenge is spending several hours every week collecting information from different systems.

Understanding that problem gives you more flexibility to explore possible solutions.

## Prioritize problems before features

You probably won't be able to address every customer problem at once.

A roadmap helps you decide where to focus your attention and resources.

When comparing opportunities, consider:

- **Customer impact:** How significantly does the problem affect the people experiencing it?
- **Frequency:** How often does the problem occur?
- **Evidence:** What have you learned from customer interviews or other research?
- **Customer segment:** Is the problem especially relevant to the group you want to serve first?
- **Current alternatives:** How are customers addressing the problem today?
- **Feasibility:** Are there technical, operational, financial, or regulatory considerations that may affect your ability to address it?
- **Business relevance:** Does addressing the problem align with the business you're trying to build?

You don't need a complex scoring system.

Start by identifying which problems appear most important and which assumptions still need to be investigated.

Avoid treating one person's feature request as proof that a particular solution should become a priority.

## Think about outcomes, not just deliverables

Traditional product roadmaps often focus on features and delivery dates.

For an early-stage venture, this can create commitments before you've gathered enough evidence to know which solutions will work.

An **outcome-based roadmap** focuses on the improvements you want to create rather than only the things you plan to build.

For example:

**Feature-focused approach:**

"Build an automated reporting dashboard."

**Outcome-focused approach:**

"Help customers reduce the time they spend preparing weekly reports."

The outcome gives you a clearer understanding of what you're trying to achieve while leaving room to explore different solutions.

Later, during the Validation stage, you can test prototypes, minimum viable products (MVPs), or other approaches to determine which solutions deliver the desired results.

## Keep your roadmap flexible

A product roadmap should provide direction without preventing you from responding to new information.

You may discover that:

- A problem is less important than you initially believed.
- A different customer segment experiences the problem more intensely.
- A proposed feature doesn't address the underlying need.
- A technical or regulatory requirement changes your priorities.
- A simpler solution could deliver the same outcome.

These discoveries may require you to change your roadmap.

That's part of the process.

**A roadmap isn't a promise to build everything you've listed. It's a tool for making informed decisions as you learn.**

At the Discovery stage, focus on clarity and priorities rather than detailed timelines.

## Business tools

### Now, next, later roadmap

A **Now, Next, Later roadmap** is a simple way to organize your priorities without committing to specific delivery dates.

It separates your work into three categories:

- **Now:** The most important problem or opportunity you're currently investigating.
- **Next:** The problems or opportunities you expect to explore after your current priorities.
- **Later:** Potential areas of focus that may become relevant as you learn more.

For example:

| Now | Next | Later |
|---|---|---|
| Understand why customers struggle to prepare weekly reports | Explore ways to reduce manual data entry | Investigate opportunities for advanced reporting |
| Identify the information customers need most often | Test possible approaches to organizing that information | Explore integrations with additional systems |

At this stage, your roadmap can include research activities, customer problems, and potential outcomes.

You don't need to assign exact dates to every item.

Review the roadmap regularly and move priorities as new evidence becomes available.

### Evidence-to-roadmap framework

Use this framework to turn your interview findings into potential roadmap priorities.

Start by reviewing your customer discovery and SME interview notes.

For each important finding, record:

1. **Customer insight:** What did you learn?
2. **Problem or need:** What underlying challenge does the insight reveal?
3. **Evidence:** Which interviews, observations, or other sources support it?
4. **Desired outcome:** What improvement would matter to the customer?
5. **Next step:** What do you need to investigate, test, or clarify?

For example:

| Customer insight | Problem or need | Desired outcome | Next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Customers manually combine information from several spreadsheets | Preparing reports requires significant time | Reduce time spent preparing reports | Investigate the reporting process in more detail |
| Customers struggle to find important records | Information is difficult to locate | Make relevant information easier to access | Explore how customers organize and retrieve records |

This framework helps you maintain a connection between your research and the priorities you include in your roadmap.

### Opportunity-solution tree

An **opportunity-solution tree** is a visual framework for connecting a desired outcome to customer needs, potential solutions, and experiments.

It typically includes:

- **Desired outcome:** The improvement you want to achieve.
- **Opportunities:** Customer needs, problems, or pain points that could help you achieve the outcome.
- **Solutions:** Possible approaches to addressing those opportunities.
- **Experiments:** Ways to test assumptions about potential solutions.

At the Discovery stage, you can begin by identifying the desired outcome and mapping the customer opportunities you've uncovered.

You don't need to select or commit to solutions immediately.

As you move into the Validation stage, you can expand the tree to include possible solutions and experiments.

This approach can help you avoid becoming attached to one idea before exploring alternatives.

### Roadmap review

Your roadmap should change as your understanding of the customer improves.

When reviewing it, ask:

- Are our priorities still supported by customer evidence?
- Have we learned anything that changes the importance of a problem?
- Are we focusing on customer outcomes or becoming attached to specific features?
- What assumptions remain untested?
- What should we investigate next?
- Are there opportunities we should remove or postpone?

Record significant changes and the reasons behind them.

This helps you understand how your product direction evolves and communicate those decisions to your team, advisors, or other stakeholders.

## Tech support

### [Miro](https://miro.com/)

Miro is a collaborative digital whiteboard that can help you organize customer insights, visualize priorities, and build product roadmaps.

You can use it to:

- Group findings from customer discovery and SME interviews
- Identify recurring problems and themes
- Create a Now, Next, Later roadmap
- Map customer opportunities and possible solutions
- Collaborate with team members and advisors

Start with a simple board rather than building a complicated planning system.

For example, create one area for interview findings, another for customer problems, and a third for your roadmap priorities.

This allows you to connect the evidence you've collected to the decisions you're making.

Review Miro's available templates, features, and sharing settings to choose an approach that fits your needs.

## Put it into practice

Create your first product roadmap using the insights you've gathered during customer discovery and SME interviews.

**Start with your research, not a list of features.**

Review your interview notes and identify three to five important customer problems or opportunities.

For each one:

1. **Identify:** Describe the problem in the customer's terms.
2. **Support:** Record the interviews or other evidence that helped you identify it.
3. **Understand:** Explain why the problem matters and who experiences it.
4. **Define:** Describe the outcome you'd like to help customers achieve.
5. **Prioritize:** Decide whether the opportunity belongs in your Now, Next, or Later category.

Use Miro or another tool you're comfortable with to organize your roadmap visually.

**Your initial roadmap might look like this:**

| Priority | Customer problem | Desired outcome | What we need to learn |
|---|---|---|---|
| Now | Customers spend too much time preparing reports | Reduce manual reporting effort | Which steps create the most difficulty? |
| Next | Customers struggle to find information | Make important information easier to access | How do customers currently organize their records? |
| Later | Customers want better visibility into performance | Help customers understand important business information | Which information matters most to their decisions? |

These are illustrative examples. Your roadmap should reflect what you've learned from your own research.

**Before considering your first roadmap ready, ask yourself:**

- Can I explain where each priority came from?
- Does my roadmap reflect real customer problems rather than only my own ideas?
- Have I considered relevant insights from subject-matter experts?
- Am I clear about which findings are supported by evidence and which are still assumptions?
- Have I identified the outcomes I want to achieve?
- Do I know what needs to be investigated or tested next?
- Is the roadmap flexible enough to change as I learn?

Your first roadmap doesn't need to be detailed or permanent.

It should help you organize what you've learned, communicate your priorities, and decide where to focus your attention.

Consider reviewing your roadmap with your TechAlliance advisor to discuss your priorities, assumptions, and next steps.

As you move into the Validation stage, you'll use your roadmap to guide the development and testing of potential solutions.

The goal is to build something that addresses a meaningful customer problem, not simply to complete a list of planned features.
