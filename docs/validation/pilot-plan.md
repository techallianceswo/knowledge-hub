# MVP/Pilot definition

## Turn your research into an offer you can test

You've spent time understanding your potential customers, investigating their problems, speaking with subject-matter experts, and exploring how your business might address those needs.

Now it's time to define what you'll actually offer your first users or customers.

During the Discovery stage, you developed an initial product roadmap and go-to-market (GTM) plan. These documents helped you identify the problems worth addressing, the customers you want to reach, and the outcomes your proposed solution should deliver.

The Validation stage is where you begin testing whether your solution works for those customers.

An **MVP (minimum viable product)** is the smallest usable version of your product that delivers meaningful value while allowing you to test important assumptions.

A **pilot** is a limited trial of your product or service with a defined group of participants, scope, and timeframe.

An MVP and a pilot aren't the same thing, but they can work together. For example, you might develop an MVP and invite a small group of customers to use it during a four-week pilot.

Your goal is to define:

- **What you're offering:** The product, service, or experience participants will receive.
- **Who it's for:** The specific customers or users you want to test it with.
- **What's included:** The essential features, services, or activities available during the test.
- **What's excluded:** The capabilities or improvements you'll leave for later.
- **How participation works:** The number of participants, duration, onboarding, and support.
- **What participants will pay:** Whether the offer is free, discounted, or paid.
- **What success looks like:** The evidence you'll collect to decide what to do next.

**An MVP or pilot isn't about launching an unfinished version of everything you hope to build. It's about offering something focused enough to test, but useful enough to create real value.**

## Start with the problem you've validated

Before defining your MVP or pilot, return to your customer discovery findings.

Ask yourself:

- Which customer problem appeared most important?
- Who experienced it most frequently or intensely?
- What are customers doing to solve it today?
- What outcomes are they trying to achieve?
- Which assumptions about the problem are supported by evidence?
- What did subject-matter experts identify as important constraints?

Your initial offer should address a specific problem for a specific group of people.

For example, imagine you've interviewed small business owners who spend several hours each week manually preparing reports.

Your long-term product vision might include automated reporting, dashboards, forecasting, integrations, and AI-generated recommendations.

But your first MVP might focus only on helping users combine information from two sources and generate one useful report.

This gives you a clearer opportunity to test whether your approach solves a meaningful problem.

## Decide whether you need an MVP, a pilot, or both

The right approach depends on your business model, solution, and what you need to learn.

**An MVP** is useful when you need a working version of your proposed solution that customers can interact with.

For example:

- A software startup releases a basic application with one essential workflow.
- A marketplace tests a limited service in one geographic area.
- A consumer product company offers a simplified first version of its product.

**A pilot** is useful when you want to test an offering under defined conditions with selected participants.

For example:

- A B2B startup works with three organizations to test a new workflow.
- A service business delivers a four-week program to a small group of clients.
- A technology company installs a solution at one customer site to assess its performance.

A pilot may use an MVP, an existing product, or a service delivered partly through manual processes.

In some cases, a prototype, demonstration, or other experiment may be more appropriate before investing in a fully usable MVP.

Choose the smallest approach that can produce meaningful evidence while meeting the safety, quality, privacy, and other requirements relevant to your offering.

## Define exactly what you're offering

Your MVP or pilot should have a clear scope.

Start by describing the experience from the participant's perspective.

What will they receive? What will they be able to accomplish? What will your team provide?

For example:

"We'll offer a four-week pilot to five independent retailers. Participants will use a basic inventory reporting tool to consolidate sales information from two sources. We'll provide onboarding and weekly support, and we'll collect feedback on usability and time spent preparing reports."

This is more useful than saying:

"We're launching an inventory management platform."

The first statement explains the target customer, the offer, its boundaries, and how the pilot will operate.

Be clear about any limitations so participants know what to expect.

## Select the features that matter most

Your product roadmap may contain many opportunities and possible features.

You don't need to include all of them in your MVP.

Start with the features or activities required to deliver the core value you're trying to test.

For each proposed feature, ask:

- Does it directly address the customer problem?
- Is it necessary for the user to complete the main task?
- Will it help us test an important assumption?
- Is there a simpler way to deliver the same value?
- Can we postpone it without undermining the test?

Distinguish between:

- **Essential features:** Required to deliver the core experience and test your main assumptions.
- **Supporting features:** Useful, but not necessary for the first version.
- **Future features:** Potential improvements that can wait until you've collected feedback.

Don't remove features or safeguards that are necessary for safety, accessibility, reliability, privacy, security, or regulatory compliance simply to make the MVP smaller.

**Minimum viable means the smallest version that can deliver and test value responsibly, not the cheapest version you can release.**

## Choose your first users or customers

Your initial participants should reflect the customer segment you've identified through Discovery.

Return to your customer segmentation research and Customer Zoom Tool.

Look for people who:

- Experience the problem you're addressing
- Match the characteristics of your intended customer segment
- Can realistically use or evaluate your offering
- Are willing to participate under clearly explained conditions
- Can provide relevant feedback or evidence about the solution

For B2B businesses, consider whether you need to involve end users, purchasing decision-makers, or both.

For B2C businesses, consider the behaviours and circumstances that make someone an appropriate early participant.

**How many participants do you need?**

There isn't one number that works for every MVP or pilot.

The right number depends on what you're testing, the variability among users, your available resources, and how much evidence you need.

A small initial pilot may be useful for observing workflows and identifying usability problems. It won't necessarily provide enough evidence to estimate market-wide demand or customer retention.

Choose a participant target you can explain, and be clear about the limits of what your results will demonstrate.

## Decide whether to charge for your pilot

One of the most important decisions is whether participants will pay to use your MVP or take part in your pilot.

There's no universal answer.

The right approach depends on your business model, what you're offering, and what you need to validate.

### Free pilot

A free pilot may make sense when:

- You're primarily testing usability or technical performance.
- The solution is still experimental.
- Participation requires significant feedback or collaboration.
- You need to reduce barriers to initial participation.

However, willingness to use something for free doesn't demonstrate willingness to pay.

If you choose a free pilot, plan how you'll investigate pricing and purchasing behaviour later.

### Discounted pilot

A discounted pilot may help you:

- Reduce the financial commitment for early customers.
- Test whether customers are willing to pay something.
- Recognize that participants are accepting limitations or contributing feedback.

Be clear about the regular price you expect to charge, if known, and what happens when the pilot ends.

### Paid pilot

A paid pilot may be appropriate when:

- The offering already delivers meaningful value.
- Customers recognize the problem and have a reason to address it.
- You're ready to test a commercial relationship.
- The scope, price, responsibilities, and expectations can be clearly defined.

A paid pilot can provide stronger evidence of willingness to pay than positive feedback alone.

However, payment by a small number of early customers doesn't automatically establish that your pricing or business model will work at scale.

Whatever approach you choose, explain the terms clearly before participation begins.

## Set a timeframe and participant expectations

A pilot should have a defined beginning and end.

Your timeline should provide enough opportunity for participants to experience the offering and for your team to collect useful evidence.

Consider:

- How long does onboarding take?
- How frequently will participants use the product or service?
- How much time is needed to observe meaningful results?
- When will you collect feedback?
- What support will your team provide?
- What happens when the pilot ends?

For example, a four-week pilot might include an onboarding session, three weeks of use, and a final feedback conversation.

That timeline may work for some products but not for others.

A solution used once a year will require a different testing approach from a tool used every day.

Set expectations about participant responsibilities, support availability, limitations, payment, data handling, and what happens after the pilot.

For B2B pilots, a written agreement may be appropriate to clarify the scope, confidentiality, data access, ownership, and other relevant terms.

## Define success before you begin

If you don't decide what you're trying to learn, it can be difficult to interpret your results.

Start with the main assumption you want to test.

For example:

"We believe independent retailers will use our reporting tool to reduce the time they spend preparing weekly reports."

Then identify what evidence would support or challenge that assumption.

You might track:

- Whether participants complete the intended task
- How often they use the offering
- How long it takes to complete the task
- Whether the solution improves the current process
- What problems participants encounter
- Whether participants continue using it
- Whether customers are willing to pay
- Whether they want to continue after the pilot

Choose metrics that reflect the value your solution is supposed to deliver.

**Success isn't simply having people sign up. It's learning whether your offering helps the right customers solve a meaningful problem.**

Decide in advance what results would lead you to continue, improve, change, or stop the approach.

## Business tools

### MVP/Pilot definition canvas

Use this canvas to bring the most important decisions about your initial offering into one place.

| Element | Your definition |
|---|---|
| Customer problem | What specific problem are you addressing? |
| Target customer | Who is the MVP or pilot for? |
| Proposed solution | What are you offering? |
| Desired outcome | What improvement should participants experience? |
| MVP or pilot format | What type of test will you conduct? |
| Essential features | What's included in the first version? |
| Exclusions | What won't be included yet? |
| Participant target | How many users or customers do you want? |
| Recruitment | How will you reach and select participants? |
| Pricing | Will participation be free, discounted, or paid? |
| Duration | How long will the test run? |
| Onboarding and support | What will participants need from your team? |
| Success measures | What evidence will you collect? |
| Constraints and risks | What requirements or limitations must you address? |
| Next decision | What will the results help you decide? |

Complete the canvas using your customer discovery findings, product roadmap, and GTM planning draft.

If you don't know an answer yet, record it as a decision or assumption that still needs attention.

### MoSCoW feature prioritization

The **MoSCoW method** helps you organize features and requirements into four categories:

- **Must have:** Essential for the MVP to work, deliver core value, or meet non-negotiable requirements.
- **Should have:** Important, but the MVP can still function without them.
- **Could have:** Useful improvements that aren't necessary for the initial test.
- **Won't have for now:** Features intentionally excluded from the current scope.

Start with the features on your product roadmap.

Assign each feature to a category based on its importance to the customer problem and the assumptions you're testing.

Be selective about what you classify as a must-have.

If nearly everything is essential, you may need to narrow the problem or customer journey you're focusing on.

### Feature value and effort matrix

Use a simple matrix to compare the value of proposed features with the effort required to deliver them.

| Feature | Customer value | Development effort | Required for the test? | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feature A | High | Low | Yes | Include |
| Feature B | High | High | Unclear | Investigate |
| Feature C | Low | Low | No | Defer |
| Feature D | Low | High | No | Exclude |

Consider whether each feature supports the core user experience or helps you collect meaningful evidence.

High-value, low-effort features may be good candidates for the first version, but some high-effort requirements may still be essential.

Use the matrix to support decisions rather than automatically excluding everything that's difficult to build.

### User story mapping

**User story mapping** helps you visualize the steps a customer takes to accomplish a goal.

Start by describing the main task your customer wants to complete.

For example:

"Prepare a weekly sales report."

Then map the steps:

1. Gather sales information.
2. Organize the data.
3. Review the results.
4. Generate a report.
5. Share the report.

Identify which steps your MVP needs to support for the customer to complete the core task.

You can then decide which features are essential for the first version and which can be added later.

This approach helps you avoid building disconnected features that don't create a complete, useful experience.

### Pilot success criteria

Before starting your pilot, define the evidence you want to collect.

| Question | Metric or evidence | Target | How you'll measure it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can users complete the main task? | Task completion | | |
| Does the solution improve their current process? | Time saved or other relevant outcome | | |
| Do participants use the offering? | Usage or participation | | |
| Is the experience workable? | Observed difficulties and feedback | | |
| Do customers see enough value to pay? | Payment or purchasing behaviour | | |
| Should we continue developing this approach? | Combined findings | | |

Set targets based on your assumptions, customer context, and the decisions you need to make.

Don't invent benchmarks or treat arbitrary numbers as universal standards.

## Put it into practice

Define your first MVP or pilot.

**By the end of this exercise, you should have a clear description of what you'll offer, who will participate, what it will cost, and how you'll evaluate the results.**

Start by reviewing your Discovery-stage research, product roadmap, and GTM planning draft.

Then:

1. **Choose your customer.** Identify the specific segment you want to serve first and explain why.
2. **Define the problem.** Describe the main customer problem your offering will address.
3. **Select your approach.** Decide whether you need an MVP, a pilot, both, or a simpler preliminary experiment.
4. **Describe the offer.** Explain what participants will receive and what they should be able to accomplish.
5. **Prioritize features.** Identify what's essential and what you'll leave for later.
6. **Set your participant target.** Decide how many users or customers you want to involve and how you'll recruit them.
7. **Choose your pricing.** Decide whether participation will be free, discounted, or paid, and explain why.
8. **Define the timeline.** Establish the duration, onboarding process, support, and participant expectations.
9. **Set success criteria.** Identify the assumptions you'll test, the evidence you'll collect, and the results that will inform your next decision.
10. **Review feasibility.** Confirm that your team can deliver the offering responsibly within its available resources.

Use the MVP/Pilot definition canvas to summarize your decisions.

**Write a short description of your initial offer using this template:**

"We're offering [PRODUCT OR SERVICE] to [TARGET CUSTOMER SEGMENT] to help them [DESIRED OUTCOME].

Our initial version will include [ESSENTIAL FEATURES OR SERVICES] and will not yet include [EXCLUDED FEATURES].

We plan to involve [NUMBER] participants over [TIMEFRAME].

Participation will be [FREE / DISCOUNTED / PAID], with [PRICE AND TERMS, IF APPLICABLE].

We'll measure success by [KEY METRICS OR EVIDENCE] to determine whether we should [NEXT DECISION]."

**Before moving forward, ask yourself:**

- Is my initial offering based on problems identified through customer discovery?
- Can I explain exactly who the MVP or pilot is for?
- Does it deliver enough value for participants to use meaningfully?
- Have I excluded features that aren't essential to the test?
- Have I decided how many participants I need and why?
- Have I made a deliberate decision about charging?
- Do I understand what it will take to deliver and support the offer?
- Have I defined what success and failure would look like?
- Will the results help me make a meaningful decision about the product?

Consider reviewing your definition with your TechAlliance advisor before committing significant resources to development or recruitment.

**Your MVP or pilot doesn't need to prove that your entire business model works. It should help you answer the next important question about whether your proposed solution delivers value to the customers you want to serve.**
