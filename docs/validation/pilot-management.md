
# MVP/Pilot testing and management

## Test your solution, measure results, and decide what comes next

You've defined your minimum viable product (MVP) or pilot, identified the customers you want to serve, and started gathering feedback through solution interviews.

Now it's time to manage your test in a real-world environment.

**MVP and pilot testing** is the process of delivering your initial offering to a defined group of customers, observing how they use it, measuring the value it creates, and using the results to guide your next decisions.

This goes beyond asking customers what they think about your solution.

You want to understand what happens when they use it over time.

Do they return? Does it solve their problem? Are they willing to pay? Can your team deliver the experience reliably?

The goal is to gather evidence that helps you decide whether to:

- **Persevere:** Continue with your current direction because the evidence supports it.
- **Iterate:** Improve specific parts of your offering based on what you've learned.
- **Pivot:** Make a meaningful change to your approach because important assumptions aren't holding up.

**A successful test isn't necessarily one where everything works. It's one that helps you make a better-informed business decision.**

## Start with a clear testing plan

Before inviting participants to use your MVP or begin a pilot, revisit your MVP/Pilot definition.

You should already have an initial understanding of:

- The customer problem you're addressing
- Your target customer segment
- The product or service you're offering
- The essential features or activities included
- The number of participants you want to involve
- Whether participation is free, discounted, or paid
- The duration of the test
- The outcomes you want to achieve

Turn these decisions into a practical testing plan.

Identify who will manage the pilot, who will support participants, how you'll collect data, and when you'll review results.

Make sure participants understand what they're receiving, what is expected of them, and how the test will work.

If your solution involves sensitive information, physical safety, regulated activities, or other significant risks, address the relevant requirements before testing begins.

You may also need agreements covering participation, confidentiality, data handling, responsibilities, or payment.

## Measure what matters to your customers

It's easy to focus on numbers that look encouraging but don't tell you whether your solution works.

For example, having 100 people register for a product doesn't necessarily mean those people are receiving value.

Instead, focus on metrics connected to the problem you're trying to solve.

If your product is designed to save time, measure whether customers actually save time.

If it's designed to simplify a process, observe whether customers can complete that process more easily.

If it's designed to improve business performance, identify which outcomes you can reasonably measure during the test.

Consider four categories of evidence.

### 1. Product usage and engagement

These metrics help you understand whether participants are using your offering.

Examples include:

- **Activation rate:** The percentage of participants who complete a meaningful first action.
- **Feature adoption:** The percentage of relevant participants who use a particular feature.
- **Usage frequency:** How often participants use the product or service.
- **Task completion rate:** The percentage of attempts in which participants successfully complete a defined task.
- **Retention:** The percentage of participants who continue using the offering over a defined period.

Choose metrics that reflect how your solution is intended to be used.

A product designed for daily use should be evaluated differently from a service customers need only once a month.

### 2. Customer outcomes

These metrics help you determine whether your solution delivers meaningful value.

Examples include:

- Time saved compared with the customer's previous approach
- Reduction in errors or rework
- Improvement in task completion
- Reduction in operating costs
- Increased productivity
- Improvement in a relevant customer experience
- Achievement of a specific business or personal goal

Where possible, record the customer's starting point before they begin using your solution.

This gives you something to compare against.

Be careful about attributing every improvement to your product. Other factors may influence the results.

### 3. Customer satisfaction and feedback

Quantitative metrics tell you what is happening. Customer conversations can help explain why.

Gather feedback about:

- What customers find useful
- What they find confusing or frustrating
- Which problems remain unresolved
- What prevents them from using the solution more often
- What they would change
- Whether they would continue using it

You can collect this information through interviews, short surveys, support conversations, and observations.

Don't rely exclusively on satisfaction ratings.

A participant may say they're satisfied while rarely using the product.

Compare what customers say with what they actually do.

### 4. Commercial viability

If your MVP or pilot involves a commercial offer, begin evaluating whether the business model could work.

Potential metrics include:

- **Paid conversion rate:** The percentage of eligible prospects or participants who become paying customers.
- **Revenue:** The money generated during the test.
- **Customer acquisition cost (CAC):** The cost of acquiring a paying customer.
- **Cost to serve:** The direct resources required to deliver and support the offering.
- **Gross margin:** Revenue remaining after the applicable direct costs of delivering the product or service.
- **Renewal or continuation rate:** The percentage of eligible customers who continue after the initial period.

Not every metric will be meaningful during a small pilot.

For example, CAC calculated from only a few customers may be highly unstable.

Focus on the information you can reliably collect and avoid drawing broad conclusions from limited data.

## Track the entire customer experience

Your test should help you understand what happens from the moment someone agrees to participate until the pilot ends.

Consider the following stages:

**Recruitment**

Are you reaching the right participants? What motivates them to join?

**Onboarding**

Can participants get started without unnecessary difficulty?

**First use**

Can they complete the main task and experience the intended value?

**Ongoing use**

Do they continue using the offering? What encourages or prevents them from returning?

**Support**

What questions, problems, or requests arise? How much effort does your team spend helping participants?

**Completion or renewal**

Do participants achieve the desired outcome? Do they want to continue using or paying for the solution?

Tracking the full experience can help you identify where customers encounter barriers.

For example, low usage may not mean customers don't value your solution.

It could mean onboarding is confusing, participants don't understand when to use it, or the product doesn't fit their existing workflow.

Investigate the cause before deciding what to change.

## Review results throughout the test

Don't wait until the pilot ends to evaluate what you've learned.

Schedule regular reviews to compare actual results with your expectations.

For a short pilot, a weekly review may be appropriate. Longer or more complex tests may require a different schedule.

During each review, ask:

- Are participants using the solution as expected?
- Are they experiencing the intended outcomes?
- What problems or complaints have emerged?
- Are there differences between customer segments?
- What have we learned about adoption and willingness to pay?
- Are our original assumptions still reasonable?
- Do we need to change anything before continuing?

Review both numbers and customer feedback.

Look for patterns rather than reacting to every individual comment.

For small samples, examine individual customer experiences as well as averages. A few unusual results can significantly influence the overall numbers.

## Make improvements without losing track of what you're testing

You may discover problems that need to be fixed during your pilot.

Some changes, such as resolving a critical bug or addressing a safety issue, shouldn't wait until the test ends.

Other changes may be better scheduled between testing rounds.

Before making a significant change, record:

- What problem prompted the change
- What evidence supports it
- What you're changing
- When the change takes effect
- Which participants will experience the new version
- What result you expect

This helps you understand whether an improvement influenced the outcome.

For example, if you change your onboarding process halfway through a pilot, compare the experiences of participants who used the original process with those who used the updated version.

Don't assume that results collected under different conditions are directly comparable.

**Improve the experience when needed, but keep a clear record of what was tested.**

## Recognize when it's time to pivot

Not every MVP or pilot will support your original assumptions.

You may discover that customers don't experience enough value, that the solution is too difficult to adopt, or that a different group of customers responds more positively.

These findings may indicate that you need to change direction.

A **pivot** is a meaningful change to an important part of your business approach based on what you've learned.

A pivot doesn't necessarily mean abandoning your business idea.

It could involve changing:

- **Customer segment:** Serving a different group of customers.
- **Customer problem:** Focusing on a different need.
- **Solution:** Using a different approach to address the problem.
- **Product scope:** Making a different capability the centre of the offering.
- **Business model:** Changing how customers pay or how the business generates revenue.
- **Distribution or acquisition channel:** Reaching customers through a different approach.

For example, imagine you've developed a reporting tool for small retailers.

During testing, you discover that retailers rarely use it, but accounting firms working with those retailers find the reporting functionality valuable.

That finding might justify investigating accounting firms as a potential customer segment.

However, one unexpected conversation isn't enough to justify a major change.

Look for consistent evidence, investigate alternative explanations, and consider what additional testing could help you make the decision.

### Persevere, iterate, or pivot?

At the end of a testing round, consider three possible directions.

**Persevere**

Continue with your current approach when the evidence suggests that your target customers are receiving meaningful value and the business opportunity remains promising.

You may still need further validation before scaling.

**Iterate**

Improve your solution when the core problem and approach appear promising, but specific features, workflows, pricing, or processes need adjustment.

**Pivot**

Reconsider a fundamental assumption when repeated evidence suggests that your current approach is unlikely to deliver the intended value or support a viable business.

Sometimes the evidence is insufficient to choose confidently.

In that case, define the next experiment needed to resolve the uncertainty rather than forcing a decision.

## Business tools

### MVP/Pilot testing dashboard

Create a simple dashboard to track the metrics most relevant to your offering.

| Category | Metric | Baseline | Target | Actual result | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Participation | Active participants | | | | |
| Activation | Participants completing the first meaningful action | | | | |
| Usage | Relevant usage frequency | | | | |
| Customer value | Main outcome achieved | | | | |
| Experience | Task completion or usability issues | | | | |
| Commercial | Paid conversions or revenue | | | | |
| Operations | Cost or effort to serve participants | | | | |

You don't need to track every metric.

Choose a small set that helps you evaluate your most important assumptions.

Define each metric clearly so everyone involved understands how it's measured.

For example:

**Activation rate = (Participants who complete the defined first meaningful action ÷ Eligible participants) × 100**

**Task completion rate = (Successfully completed task attempts ÷ Total valid task attempts) × 100**

**Paid conversion rate = (Participants who become paying customers ÷ Eligible participants offered a paid conversion) × 100**

Use consistent definitions and time periods when comparing results.

### Assumption and experiment tracker

Use this tool to connect your original assumptions to the evidence collected during testing.

| Assumption | Test or observation | Success criteria | Actual evidence | Decision |

| The solution improves their current process | | | | |
| Participants will continue using the solution | | | | |
| Customers will consider paying for the offering | | | | |

Review the tracker throughout the pilot.

If an assumption isn't supported, investigate why before deciding what to change.

### Weekly pilot review

Use a recurring review to keep your team focused on learning and improvement.

**1. Review the numbers**

Compare actual results with your targets and previous observations.

**2. Review customer feedback**

Identify recurring problems, requests, and positive experiences.

**3. Investigate differences**

Explore why some participants are more successful or engaged than others.

**4. Identify priorities**

Choose the most important issues to address.

**5. Assign actions**

Record who is responsible for each next step and when it should happen.

**6. Document decisions**

Explain what you've decided, what evidence informed the decision, and what you expect to learn next.

Keep a record of each review so you can see how your understanding changes over time.

### Build-measure-learn loop

The **Build-Measure-Learn** framework is a way to improve a solution through repeated experiments.

It involves three connected activities:

1. **Build:** Create or adjust the smallest offering or change needed to test an assumption.
2. **Measure:** Observe how customers respond and collect relevant evidence.
3. **Learn:** Decide what the evidence means and what to do next.

The process then repeats.

For example:

**Build:** Simplify your MVP's onboarding process.

**Measure:** Track whether new participants complete onboarding and reach the first meaningful action.

**Learn:** Determine whether the change improved activation or whether another barrier remains.

Use the framework to make purposeful changes rather than adding features without a clear reason.

### Pivot decision framework

Before making a significant change, summarize the evidence.

| Question | Your findings |
|---|---|
| What assumption is being challenged? | |
| What evidence suggests it isn't holding up? | |
| How many relevant customers or situations support the finding? | |
| What alternative explanations have we considered? | |
| What new direction are we considering? | |
| What evidence supports that direction? | |
| What would we need to test next? | |
| What would it cost to continue versus change direction? | |

Use this framework to structure a discussion with your team or TechAlliance advisor.

A pivot should be a deliberate response to evidence, not simply a reaction to disappointing results.

## Tech support

### [Google Sheets](https://sheets.google.com/)

Google Sheets can help you build a pilot dashboard, track participants, calculate metrics, and maintain an assumption and experiment tracker.

It can also help you compare results across different testing periods.

### [Microsoft Excel](https://www.microsoft.com/en-ca/microsoft-365/excel)

Microsoft Excel can help you organize testing data, analyze results, and create charts showing changes in usage, customer outcomes, or operational performance.

### [Google Forms](https://forms.google.com/)

Google Forms can help you collect structured feedback from pilot participants.

Use short surveys at relevant points in the experience, such as after onboarding or at the end of the pilot.

Surveys should complement direct observations and usage data rather than replace them.

### [PostHog](https://posthog.com/)

PostHog provides product analytics tools that can help digital product teams understand user behaviour.

Depending on your implementation, you may be able to track events such as onboarding completion, feature usage, and customer journeys.

Define the behaviours you need to measure before implementing analytics.

Configure data collection with appropriate privacy, consent, and security considerations.

### [Microsoft Clarity](https://clarity.microsoft.com/)

Microsoft Clarity provides tools for understanding how people interact with websites, including heatmaps and session recordings.

It may help you identify usability issues and areas where users encounter difficulty.

Review privacy requirements and configure the tool appropriately before collecting visitor interaction data.

### [Trello](https://trello.com/)

Trello can help you manage issues, improvement requests, and tasks that emerge during testing.

Create a simple workflow for recording, prioritizing, assigning, and completing changes.

## Put it into practice

Manage an MVP or pilot testing round from start to finish.

**Your goal is to collect enough relevant evidence to make an informed decision about your solution and the next steps for your business.**

Start by reviewing your MVP/Pilot definition and solution interview findings.

Then:

1. **Confirm your assumptions.** Identify the most important things you need to validate about customer value, usability, adoption, and commercial potential.
2. **Choose your metrics.** Select a small set of indicators directly connected to those assumptions.
3. **Record your baseline.** Document how customers currently address the problem and what outcomes they experience, where measurable.
4. **Prepare the test.** Confirm participants, responsibilities, timelines, onboarding, support, and relevant safeguards.
5. **Launch the MVP or pilot.** Give participants access to the offering and explain what they can expect.
6. **Monitor activity.** Track usage, customer outcomes, support needs, and any operational problems.
7. **Collect feedback.** Speak with participants regularly to understand what is and isn't working.
8. **Review results.** Hold periodic reviews to compare evidence with your expectations.
9. **Document changes.** Record improvements made during the test and their potential effects.
10. **Make a decision.** Determine whether to persevere, iterate, pivot, or conduct another focused experiment.

**At the end of the test, prepare a short summary covering:**

- What you intended to validate
- Who participated and how the test was conducted
- What you measured
- What actually happened
- What customers said and did
- Which assumptions were supported or challenged
- What changes were made during the test
- What you recommend doing next

**Before making your next decision, ask yourself:**

- Did the solution address the problem we intended to solve?
- Did participants experience measurable value?
- Did they use the solution as expected?
- What prevented some participants from adopting or continuing to use it?
- Did we learn anything meaningful about willingness to pay?
- Can we deliver the offering reliably and at a reasonable cost?
- Which assumptions remain uncertain?
- Do the results support continuing, improving, or changing our approach?
- What is the most important thing we need to test next?

Consider reviewing your results with your TechAlliance advisor before committing significant resources to further development or growth.

**Remember:** Validation isn't about proving that your original idea was right.

It's about gathering evidence, learning from real customer behaviour, and making informed decisions about what to build, improve, or change.

The strongest outcome of an MVP or pilot is a clearer understanding of whether your solution creates meaningful customer value and what your business should do next.
