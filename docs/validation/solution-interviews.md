# Solution interviews

## Put your MVP or pilot in front of real customers

You've identified a customer problem, gathered insights from potential customers, and defined the minimum viable product (MVP) or pilot you want to offer.

Now it's time to see how potential customers respond to your solution.

A **solution interview** is a structured conversation where you introduce your MVP or pilot to a potential customer, let them experience its core functionality or service, and learn whether it addresses their needs.

Unlike customer discovery interviews, which focus on understanding the problem, solution interviews focus on evaluating your proposed solution.

The goal is to understand:

- **Usability:** Can customers understand and use your solution?
- **Value:** Does it address the problem they experience?
- **Relevance:** Does it fit their needs and existing workflows?
- **Adoption:** What would encourage or prevent them from using it?
- **Features:** Which capabilities are essential, missing, or unnecessary?
- **Willingness to pay:** Would customers consider paying for the value it provides?

Whenever possible, let participants interact with your actual MVP or experience the service you're proposing.

Observe what they do, where they encounter difficulties, and what questions they ask.

**Don't just ask customers whether they like your solution. Give them an opportunity to use it and learn from their behaviour.**

## Start with a clear learning objective

Before scheduling solution interviews, return to your MVP/Pilot definition and identify the assumptions you need to test.

For example:

"We believe independent retailers can use our reporting tool to reduce the time they spend preparing weekly sales reports."

Your interview should help you understand whether customers can use the tool, whether it addresses their problem, and what might prevent them from adopting it.

You might want to investigate:

- Can customers complete the main task using your solution?
- Does the solution address the problem identified during customer discovery?
- What parts of the experience are confusing or difficult?
- Which features do customers actually use?
- What capabilities are missing?
- How does the solution compare with their current approach?
- Would customers consider adopting or paying for it?

Choose a small number of learning objectives for each round of interviews.

Trying to evaluate every aspect of your product at once can make it difficult to identify what matters most.

## Interview people who experience the problem

Solution interviews are most useful when participants match the customer segment you're trying to serve.

Return to your customer discovery findings and Customer Zoom Tool to identify people who experience the problem and are likely to benefit from your offering.

You can invite people who participated in your earlier customer discovery interviews.

However, it's also useful to include new participants who haven't previously seen your solution.

This can help you understand whether someone can recognize its value without already being familiar with your business idea.

For B2B solutions, consider whether you need to speak with:

- **End users:** People who will interact with the product or service.
- **Decision-makers:** People who approve purchases or decide whether a solution can be adopted.
- **Other stakeholders:** People responsible for implementation, technical requirements, security, or operations.

Different stakeholders may have different expectations and concerns.

For B2C solutions, focus on people whose needs, behaviours, and circumstances match your intended customer segment.

Avoid relying only on friends, family members, or people who are already enthusiastic about your idea.

## Let customers experience the solution

The most useful feedback often comes from observing someone interact with your product or service.

Instead of walking participants through every feature, give them an opportunity to accomplish a realistic task.

For example, if you've developed a reporting tool, you might say:

"Imagine you need to prepare your weekly sales report using this tool. Show me how you would approach it."

Let the participant explore the experience.

Pay attention to:

- What they try to do first
- Whether they understand the available options
- Where they hesitate or become confused
- Which features they use or ignore
- Whether they complete the task
- When they ask for help
- How their experience compares with their existing process

Avoid immediately correcting mistakes or explaining how something should work.

If a participant gets stuck, give them time to work through the problem before offering assistance.

Record where assistance was necessary.

For service-based businesses, the same principle applies.

Rather than only describing your service, let participants experience the part of the service you're trying to validate.

For example, a consulting business might deliver a limited advisory session, while a training company might offer an initial workshop.

The goal is to observe how customers experience the value you're proposing.

## Ask questions without influencing the answers

During a solution interview, your role is to learn rather than persuade.

It's natural to want participants to appreciate the work you've put into your solution.

However, explaining why a feature is valuable or defending your design decisions can influence their feedback.

Avoid questions such as:

- "Don't you think this feature makes things easier?"
- "Wouldn't you prefer this over your current process?"
- "You'd use this regularly, right?"

Instead, ask:

- "What were you expecting to happen?"
- "What made that step difficult?"
- "How would you normally complete this task?"
- "What would you change about this experience?"
- "What would prevent you from using this?"

When someone makes a general statement, ask for more detail.

For example, if a participant says, "This is really useful," you might ask:

"What part of the experience would make the biggest difference to you?"

If they say, "I wouldn't use this feature," ask:

"Can you tell me what makes it less relevant to your needs?"

**Focus on understanding why customers respond the way they do, not convincing them that your approach is correct.**

## Explore whether the solution delivers value

A product can be easy to use without solving a meaningful problem.

After participants experience your MVP or pilot, explore whether it improves their current situation.

Ask questions such as:

- Did this help you accomplish what you needed?
- How does this compare with your current approach?
- What would make this more useful in your daily work or life?
- What would you still need to do outside this solution?
- What would prevent you from using it again?

Whenever possible, connect their feedback to observable outcomes.

For example, if your solution is intended to save time, compare how long the task takes using the MVP with the customer's current process.

If your solution is intended to reduce errors, investigate whether participants complete the task more accurately.

If your solution is intended to simplify a process, observe whether they can complete it with fewer steps or less assistance.

Choose evidence that reflects the value your product is supposed to deliver.

Positive comments can be encouraging, but measurable improvements and actual customer behaviour provide stronger evidence.

## Explore adoption and willingness to pay

Even when customers recognize the value of a solution, they may face barriers to adopting it.

These could include:

- The cost of switching from an existing solution
- The time required for onboarding or training
- Compatibility with existing tools or processes
- Security, privacy, or regulatory requirements
- Approval from managers or purchasing teams
- Concerns about reliability or ongoing support
- The price of the product or service

Ask participants what would need to happen before they could realistically adopt your solution.

If you're considering charging for your MVP or pilot, explore how the proposed price compares with their current alternatives.

For example:

"If this solution were available for [PRICE] under [TERMS], how would that compare with what you currently spend to address this problem?"

Follow up to understand their reasoning.

Remember that saying "I'd pay for this" isn't the same as making a purchase.

Stronger signals may include:

- Agreeing to participate in a paid pilot
- Requesting a formal proposal
- Introducing you to a purchasing decision-maker
- Committing resources to implementation
- Making an actual purchase

The significance of each action depends on your business model and the circumstances.

**Your goal is to understand what customers are willing to do, not just what they say they might do.**

## Use what you learn to improve your MVP or pilot

After several solution interviews, review your findings and identify recurring patterns.

You may discover that:

- Customers struggle to complete an important task.
- A feature you considered essential isn't being used.
- Participants need a capability you hadn't included.
- The solution doesn't fit their existing workflow.
- Customers recognize value but face barriers to adoption.
- A different customer segment responds more positively.

Use these insights to update your MVP/Pilot definition and product roadmap.

Avoid adding every feature someone requests.

Before making changes, investigate the underlying problem and consider whether the proposed improvement supports your core value proposition.

Some findings may require small adjustments. Others may suggest that you need to reconsider your approach.

That's part of validation.

## Business tools

### Solution interview guide

Use the following questions as a starting point for your interviews.

Adapt them to your customer segment, MVP or pilot, and learning objectives.

**1. Revisit the problem**

Before introducing your solution, briefly confirm the participant's current experience.

- How are you currently handling [SPECIFIC TASK OR PROBLEM]?
- What's the most challenging part of that process?
- What tools or approaches do you use today?
- Has anything changed since we last discussed this problem?

**2. Introduce the MVP or pilot**

Briefly explain what the participant will be doing.

Avoid providing detailed instructions unless they're necessary for safety or the nature of the task.

- Here's the product or service we're testing.
- I'd like you to try completing [SPECIFIC TASK].
- Please approach it as you normally would.
- Feel free to describe what you're thinking as you go.

**3. Observe the experience**

Let the participant interact with your solution.

Use neutral follow-up questions when appropriate.

- What are you trying to do right now?
- What were you expecting to happen?
- What made you choose that option?
- Is anything unclear?
- What would you normally do at this point?

**4. Explore value and usability**

After the participant has completed the experience, ask:

- What worked well?
- What was difficult or confusing?
- Were you able to accomplish what you needed?
- How does this compare with your current approach?
- Which parts were most useful?
- What felt unnecessary?
- What would need to change before you could use this regularly?

**5. Explore adoption and pricing**

- What would prevent you from adopting this solution?
- What would need to happen before you considered switching?
- Who else would be involved in that decision?
- How would the proposed price compare with your current alternatives?
- What would be a realistic next step if you wanted to continue using it?

**6. Close the conversation**

- What's your biggest concern about this solution?
- Is there anything important I haven't asked?
- Would you be interested in participating in a longer pilot or another round of testing?

You don't need to ask every question.

Focus on your learning objectives and allow the participant's experience to guide the conversation.

### Solution validation hypothesis

Before each interview round, write down the main assumption you want to test.

Use the following structure:

**We believe** [TARGET CUSTOMER] will be able to use [MVP OR PILOT] to achieve [DESIRED OUTCOME].

**We'll test this by** asking [TARGET PARTICIPANTS] to complete [REALISTIC TASK OR EXPERIENCE].

**We'll look for evidence that** [OBSERVABLE BEHAVIOURS OR MEASURABLE RESULTS].

**We may need to reconsider our approach if** [RESULTS THAT CHALLENGE THE ASSUMPTION].

For example:

"We believe independent retailers will be able to use our reporting tool to prepare their weekly sales reports with less manual work.

We'll test this by asking retailers to prepare a report using the MVP.

We'll look for evidence that participants can complete the task, require limited assistance, and spend less time than they do with their current process.

We may need to reconsider our approach if participants struggle to complete the task or don't experience a meaningful improvement."

Define your expectations before the interviews so you can evaluate the results more objectively.

### Feature feedback matrix

After conducting several interviews, compare what you've learned about your MVP's features.

| Feature | Customer need addressed | Observed use | Feedback | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Feature A | | | | Keep / Improve / Remove |
| Feature B | | | | Keep / Improve / Remove |
| Feature C | | | | Keep / Improve / Remove |

Look for patterns such as:

- Features customers consistently use to complete important tasks
- Capabilities that cause confusion or require assistance
- Features that don't appear to contribute to the core value
- Missing capabilities that prevent task completion
- Requests that reveal an underlying customer need

Use these findings to refine your MVP scope.

Don't automatically prioritize a feature because several people requested it.

Consider whether it supports the main problem you're trying to solve and whether the feedback comes from customers who match your target segment.

### Interview findings synthesis

After completing your initial interviews, organize your findings into four categories:

**1. What works**

Which parts of the MVP or pilot help customers accomplish their goals?

**2. What needs improvement**

Where do participants encounter difficulties, confusion, or barriers?

**3. What may not be necessary**

Which features or activities don't appear to contribute to the core value?

**4. What remains uncertain**

Which assumptions require more interviews, additional product usage, or another experiment?

Use the summary to identify the changes that matter most.

Separate improvements that are essential for the next round of testing from ideas that can wait.

## Tech support

### [Google Meet](https://meet.google.com/)

Google Meet can help you conduct remote solution interviews while participants interact with your MVP or pilot.

Screen sharing can be useful for observing how customers navigate a digital product or complete a task.

If you plan to record the session, obtain the participant's permission and review the available recording and privacy settings.

### [Microsoft Teams](https://www.microsoft.com/en-ca/microsoft-teams/group-chat-software)

Microsoft Teams is another option for conducting remote interviews and observing participants as they interact with your solution.

It may be particularly useful when interviewing business customers who already use Microsoft tools.

### [Google Forms](https://forms.google.com/)

Google Forms can help you collect structured feedback after participants have experienced your MVP or pilot.

For example, you can ask participants about task difficulty, perceived value, and barriers to adoption.

Keep forms brief and use them to complement interviews rather than replace direct observation.

### [Google Sheets](https://sheets.google.com/)

Google Sheets can help you organize interview notes, track task completion, compare feature feedback, and identify recurring problems.

Use a consistent observation template so you can compare results across participants.

### [ChatGPT](https://chatgpt.com/)

ChatGPT can help you organize anonymized interview notes, identify recurring themes, and summarize potential improvements to your MVP or pilot.

You can also use it to review your interview questions for leading language or unclear wording.

Review AI-generated summaries against your original observations before making product decisions.

Avoid uploading identifiable customer information, confidential business details, or recordings without appropriate consent and safeguards.

## Put it into practice

Conduct your first round of solution interviews using the MVP or pilot you've defined.

**Your goal is to observe how potential customers experience your solution and identify what needs to change before expanding its use.**

Start by reviewing your MVP/Pilot definition and selecting the most important assumptions you want to test.

Then:

1. **Define your learning objective.** Identify the customer problem, desired outcome, and assumptions you're investigating.
2. **Prepare your MVP or pilot.** Make sure participants can experience the core value you're trying to test. Confirm that relevant safety, privacy, and other requirements are addressed.
3. **Recruit participants.** Select people who match your target customer segment and experience the problem.
4. **Prepare a realistic task.** Decide what participants should try to accomplish using your solution.
5. **Conduct the interview.** Let participants interact with the offering while you observe their behaviour.
6. **Explore their experience.** Ask neutral questions about usability, value, missing capabilities, and adoption barriers.
7. **Investigate willingness to pay.** Where relevant, discuss pricing and look for meaningful next actions.
8. **Document your observations.** Record task completion, difficulties, feedback, and unexpected behaviours.
9. **Compare findings.** Identify recurring patterns across participants.
10. **Refine your solution.** Update your MVP/Pilot definition and product roadmap based on what you've learned.

**Aim to begin with five relevant participants**, then conduct additional interviews if important questions remain unanswered.

Five interviews are a practical starting point for qualitative learning, not a universal threshold for validating your solution.

**After completing your interviews, ask yourself:**

- Could participants use the MVP or experience the pilot as intended?
- Were they able to complete the main task?
- Where did they struggle or require assistance?
- Did the solution improve their current approach?
- Which features were essential to delivering value?
- Which features were confusing, unnecessary, or missing?
- What barriers to adoption did participants identify?
- Did anyone demonstrate interest through a meaningful next action?
- What did I learn about pricing and purchasing decisions?
- What should I change before involving more customers?

Summarize your findings and identify your next steps.

You may need to fix usability problems, adjust features, reconsider your pricing, or conduct another round of interviews.

Consider reviewing your findings with your TechAlliance advisor to discuss which assumptions are supported and which need further testing.

**Remember:** Solution interviews provide valuable evidence about how customers experience your MVP or pilot, but one successful session doesn't mean your solution is fully validated.

As you continue through the Validation stage, look for stronger evidence through repeated use, measurable customer outcomes, retention, and actual purchasing behaviour.

The goal is to move from a solution that customers understand to one that consistently delivers value.
