
# Sales assets and process

## Turn your validated solution into a repeatable sales process

You've tested your solution with potential customers, collected feedback, and started understanding what makes people willing to use or pay for your offering.

Now it's time to build a sales process that helps you reach more of the right customers, communicate your value, and turn opportunities into revenue.

During the Discovery stage, you developed a draft go-to-market (GTM) plan. During Validation, you tested your offering and learned more about customer needs, adoption, and willingness to pay.

The Efficiency stage is where you use that evidence to establish more consistent business operations.

**A sales process is a defined series of activities your business follows to identify potential customers, understand their needs, present an appropriate solution, and guide them toward a purchasing decision.**

**Sales assets** are the materials and tools that support those activities, such as your website, product descriptions, sales presentations, proposals, and customer relationship management (CRM) system.

Together, they help you:

- Explain what you sell and who it's for
- Reach and qualify potential customers
- Communicate the value of your offering
- Manage opportunities and follow-ups
- Coordinate responsibilities across your team
- Track sales performance
- Improve your approach as you learn

Your first sales process doesn't need to be complicated.

It should be clear enough that someone else could understand how your business sells, follow the steps, and identify what needs improvement.

**The goal isn't to create more paperwork. It's to make sales more consistent, measurable, and manageable as your business grows.**

## Make sure your business is ready to sell

Before setting ambitious sales targets, consider whether your business can deliver what it sells.

More customers can create new operational challenges, particularly when your team is small.

Start by reviewing your business goals and operating capacity.

Ask yourself:

- What are our revenue and growth goals?
- How many customers can we currently serve?
- What would happen if our sales doubled?
- Do we have enough time, people, equipment, and resources to deliver?
- What additional costs would growth create?
- At what point would we need to hire, purchase equipment, or expand our operations?
- Can we maintain the quality of our offering as demand increases?

For example, a consulting business might be able to serve five additional clients without hiring anyone.

However, serving 20 additional clients could require new employees, software, or other resources.

A product business might face different constraints, such as manufacturing capacity, inventory, infrastructure, or customer support.

Understanding these limits helps you set realistic sales goals and avoid making commitments your business can't fulfil.

If you haven't already documented your business model, consider completing a Business Model Canvas or Lean Canvas.

These tools can help you connect your sales strategy with the rest of your business.

## Define your ideal customer profile

You may have identified several potential customer segments during Discovery.

Your Validation-stage findings should now help you understand which customers are most likely to benefit from your solution.

Use that evidence to refine your **ideal customer profile (ICP)**.

An ICP describes the characteristics of customers who are a particularly good fit for your offering.

For B2B businesses, these characteristics might include:

- Industry
- Company size
- Geographic location
- Business model
- Operational challenges
- Existing technology or processes
- Purchasing capacity
- Relevant growth stage or business circumstances

For B2C businesses, your customer profile may focus more on behaviours, needs, circumstances, and purchasing patterns.

Avoid defining your ICP so broadly that nearly everyone qualifies.

A focused customer profile helps you prioritize opportunities and tailor your sales approach.

### Identify who makes purchasing decisions

The person who uses your solution isn't always the person who decides to buy it.

For B2B sales, you may need to engage several stakeholders.

For example:

- **End user:** The person who will use your product or service.
- **Decision-maker:** The person with authority to approve the purchase.
- **Influencer:** Someone who helps evaluate or recommend solutions.
- **Budget owner:** The person responsible for the relevant spending.
- **Technical or operational stakeholder:** Someone who assesses implementation, compatibility, security, or other requirements.

These roles may overlap, particularly in smaller organizations.

Understanding the purchasing process helps you avoid spending time on opportunities that cannot move forward.

Ask who experiences the problem, who benefits from solving it, who approves spending, and what steps are required before a purchase can happen.

## Set sales goals that match your capacity

Once you've defined your target customers and operating capacity, establish initial sales goals.

Consider:

- How much revenue do you want to generate monthly or quarterly?
- How many customers or contracts would that require?
- What is your expected average sale value?
- How long does it typically take to close a sale?
- How many opportunities would you need to reach your target?
- Can your business deliver that volume profitably?

For example, if your quarterly revenue target is $30,000 and your average sale is $5,000, you would need six completed sales to reach that target.

The next question is how many qualified opportunities you'll need to generate those six sales.

If you don't have enough sales history to estimate conversion rates, start with assumptions and update them as you collect data.

Don't treat early estimates as established benchmarks.

Your sales targets should reflect both your commercial ambitions and your ability to deliver.

## Create the sales assets your customers need

Sales assets help potential customers understand your offering and make purchasing decisions.

You don't need to create every possible asset immediately.

Start with the materials that support your actual sales process.

### Website and landing pages

Your website may be the first place a potential customer learns about your business.

Consider how someone unfamiliar with your company would experience it.

Can they quickly understand:

- What you offer?
- Who it's for?
- What problem it solves?
- What benefits they can expect?
- Why they should consider your solution?
- What they should do next?

Use clear, customer-focused language.

Avoid relying on technical terminology or feature lists without explaining the value.

If you serve different customer segments, you may need dedicated pages that address their specific needs.

Make it easy for visitors to take the next step, such as requesting a demonstration, booking a conversation, or making a purchase.

### Product and service descriptions

Create a clear description of each product or service you're ready to sell.

Include:

- The customer problem it addresses
- The intended customer
- What the offering includes
- The main benefits or expected outcomes
- Important limitations or requirements
- Pricing or how to request a quote
- The next step to purchase or learn more

Focus on the value customers receive.

For example, instead of only saying:

"Our software includes automated data integration."

Explain the customer benefit:

"Bring sales information from multiple systems into one report, reducing the manual work involved in preparing weekly updates."

Support claims about savings, performance, or other outcomes with evidence where available.

### Sales pitch

Your sales pitch should help you explain your offering clearly and confidently.

Unlike an investor pitch, a customer-facing sales pitch should focus on the customer's problem, your solution, and the value of addressing that problem.

Prepare different versions for situations such as:

- A brief introduction at a networking event
- An initial outreach message
- A discovery or qualification call
- A product demonstration
- A conversation with a purchasing decision-maker

Use your earlier elevator pitch work as a starting point, but adapt it to the customer's context.

### Sales presentation

A sales deck can help structure conversations with potential customers, especially for B2B products and services.

A useful presentation might cover:

1. The customer problem
2. The impact of leaving that problem unresolved
3. Your proposed solution
4. How the product or service works
5. The benefits and outcomes
6. What makes your approach different
7. Relevant evidence from customer testing or actual results
8. The implementation or purchasing process
9. The next step

Keep the presentation focused on the audience.

Don't overload it with company history or technical details that aren't relevant to the purchasing decision.

### Proposals, quotes, and pricing information

If your business requires customized proposals or quotes, prepare a reusable template.

Depending on your offering, it might include:

- Customer needs and objectives
- Proposed scope of work
- Deliverables
- Timeline
- Pricing and payment terms
- Responsibilities
- Assumptions and exclusions
- Acceptance or approval process

Make sure your pricing reflects your costs, intended margins, and the value you're delivering.

Review significant legal or contractual terms with an appropriate professional when necessary.

### Supporting materials

Depending on your business, you may also benefit from:

- Product demonstration scripts
- Frequently asked questions
- Onboarding information
- Comparison sheets
- Technical specifications
- Security or compliance information
- Case studies or testimonials
- Follow-up email templates

Only use testimonials, performance claims, and customer examples that are genuine and approved for the intended use.

**Prioritize the assets customers need to understand, evaluate, and purchase your offering. Add more materials as your sales process develops.**

## Choose how you'll reach potential customers

Return to your GTM planning draft and consider which channels showed the most promise during Validation.

Your sales channels are the ways potential customers discover your offering or connect with your team.

Examples include:

- LinkedIn outreach
- Email outreach
- Networking events
- Industry associations
- Referrals
- Strategic partnerships
- In-person visits
- Website inquiries
- Search engines
- Social media
- Paid advertising

Not every channel will be appropriate for your business.

A specialized B2B technology company may benefit from targeted outreach and industry partnerships.

A consumer product business may rely more heavily on online discovery, retail distribution, or direct purchasing.

Start with a manageable number of channels that match your customers' behaviour and your team's resources.

For each channel, define:

- Who you're trying to reach
- Why the channel is appropriate
- What message or offer you'll use
- What action you want the prospect to take
- Who will manage the activity
- How you'll measure the results

Follow applicable privacy, consent, and anti-spam requirements when conducting outreach.

Your channel strategy determines how potential customers enter your sales funnel.

Your sales process determines what happens next.

## Define your sales funnel and process

A **sales funnel** describes how potential customers move from initial awareness toward a purchase.

A **sales pipeline** tracks individual opportunities as they progress through defined stages.

A **sales process** describes the actions your team takes to help customers move through those stages.

For example, a B2B sales process might include:

| Stage | Objective | Typical activities |
|---|---|---|
| Lead generation | Identify potential customers | Research, outreach, referrals, inbound inquiries |
| Qualification | Determine whether there's a potential fit | Understand needs, budget, decision-makers, and timing |
| Discovery | Understand the customer's situation | Ask questions, identify requirements, clarify desired outcomes |
| Presentation or demonstration | Show how your solution addresses the need | Tailored pitch, product demonstration, relevant materials |
| Proposal or quote | Present a commercial offer | Scope, pricing, timeline, terms |
| Negotiation and decision | Address questions and reach a decision | Follow-ups, approvals, contract discussions |
| Closed won or lost | Record the outcome | Confirm purchase or document why the opportunity didn't proceed |
| Customer handoff | Prepare for successful delivery | Share commitments, requirements, and onboarding information |

Your business may need fewer or different stages.

For example, an online store may have a largely self-service purchasing process.

A complex enterprise solution may involve several demonstrations, technical reviews, and procurement steps.

Design the process around how your customers actually buy.

### Define what happens at each stage

For every stage, establish:

- What qualifies an opportunity to enter the stage
- What actions your team should take
- What information needs to be collected
- Which sales assets should be used
- Who is responsible
- What must happen before the opportunity moves forward
- How and when to follow up

This prevents opportunities from getting lost or remaining in the pipeline without a clear next step.

## Assign sales responsibilities

A process only works when someone is responsible for executing it.

In an early-stage startup, the founder may manage most sales activities.

As the business grows, responsibilities may be shared among co-founders, employees, contractors, or dedicated sales staff.

Consider who will:

- Identify and research leads
- Conduct outreach
- Qualify opportunities
- Deliver presentations and demonstrations
- Prepare proposals
- Negotiate terms
- Follow up with prospects
- Update the CRM
- Manage the customer handoff
- Review sales performance

Make responsibilities explicit, even if one person handles several tasks.

If you're considering hiring someone to support sales, document your process before expecting them to execute it independently.

## Set up your CRM

A **customer relationship management (CRM) system** helps you organize information about prospects, customers, and sales opportunities.

At an early stage, a well-maintained spreadsheet may be sufficient.

As your sales activity grows, dedicated CRM software can make collaboration, follow-ups, and reporting easier.

At minimum, you should be able to track:

- Company or customer name
- Contact information
- Customer segment
- Lead source
- Current sales stage
- Estimated opportunity value
- Expected decision or closing date
- Last interaction
- Next action and due date
- Person responsible
- Final outcome and reason, when known

Keep information accurate and up to date.

Limit access to appropriate team members and handle personal information according to applicable privacy requirements.

A CRM is only useful when your team consistently records and uses the information.

## Document your process in a sales SOP

Once you've defined your sales activities, bring everything together in a **standard operating procedure (SOP)**.

An SOP is a practical document explaining how a recurring business process should be performed.

Your sales SOP should help someone understand how to manage an opportunity from initial contact to a completed sale or other outcome.

Include:

- Sales goals and target customer profile
- Lead generation channels
- Qualification criteria
- Sales stages and their definitions
- Responsibilities
- Outreach and follow-up procedures
- Required sales assets
- CRM instructions
- Proposal and approval procedures
- Customer handoff steps
- Performance metrics
- Review and improvement schedule

Keep it practical.

A short document with clear steps and links to useful templates is often more valuable than a lengthy manual nobody uses.

Review the SOP as your sales process evolves.

## Measure and improve your sales performance

Once your process is running, begin tracking how opportunities move through your funnel.

Useful metrics include:

**Sales activity**

- Number of prospects contacted
- Number of responses received
- Number of discovery calls completed
- Number of demonstrations delivered
- Number of proposals sent

**Conversion**

- Percentage of leads that become qualified opportunities
- Percentage of qualified opportunities that reach the proposal stage
- Percentage of proposals that become sales
- Overall win rate

**Revenue and efficiency**

- Revenue generated
- Average deal value
- Average sales cycle length
- Cost of acquiring a customer
- Revenue or margin relative to sales effort

**Customer insights**

- Reasons opportunities are lost
- Common objections
- Frequently requested features
- Differences between customer segments
- Feedback about pricing or implementation

Don't measure activity alone.

Sending more messages isn't necessarily an improvement if those messages aren't reaching appropriate customers or generating meaningful opportunities.

Review your results regularly.

For example, if many prospects agree to meetings but few request proposals, investigate what happens during those conversations.

You may need to adjust your qualification criteria, presentation, offering, or customer profile.

If many proposals are sent but few are accepted, investigate pricing, timing, decision-making requirements, or how well the proposal addresses customer needs.

**Use sales data to identify where your process needs attention, then make focused improvements and measure the results.**

### Sales SOP template

Create one document that explains how your company manages sales.

Use the following structure:

**1. Purpose and goals**

What is the process intended to achieve?

**2. Target customers**

Who are you selling to, and how do you recognize a qualified opportunity?

**3. Sales channels**

Where do leads come from, and how are they contacted?

**4. Sales stages**

What are the steps from initial contact to purchase?

**5. Activities and responsibilities**

What must happen at each stage, and who is responsible?

**6. Sales assets**

Which templates, presentations, scripts, and other materials should be used?

**7. CRM procedures**

What information must be recorded and updated?

**8. Customer handoff**

How are commitments and customer requirements transferred to the person or team delivering the offering?

**9. Performance measurement**

Which metrics will be tracked, and how often will results be reviewed?

**10. Continuous improvement**

How will the process be updated based on performance and customer feedback?

## Put it into practice

Build the first version of your company's sales process and prepare the assets needed to execute it.

**By the end of this exercise, you should have a documented sales process, a clear set of supporting materials, and a way to track opportunities and measure results.**

Start by reviewing your GTM planning draft, MVP/Pilot findings, and customer feedback.

Then:

1. **Review your business goals and capacity.** Determine how much you want to sell and what your team can realistically deliver.
2. **Refine your ideal customer profile.** Identify the customers most likely to benefit from your solution and the people involved in purchasing decisions.
3. **Set initial sales targets.** Define revenue or customer acquisition goals for a realistic period.
4. **Review your offering.** Make sure you can clearly explain what you're selling, what it includes, and why it matters to customers.
5. **Prepare essential sales assets.** Update your website, product descriptions, sales pitch, presentation, and proposal templates as needed.
6. **Choose your sales channels.** Identify how you'll reach potential customers and generate opportunities.
7. **Map your sales funnel.** Define the stages a prospect moves through before becoming a customer.
8. **Assign responsibilities.** Decide who will manage each activity and follow-up.
9. **Set up your CRM.** Create a consistent way to record prospects, opportunities, interactions, and next steps.
10. **Document your SOP.** Bring your sales process into one practical document that your team can follow.
11. **Start executing.** Use the process with real prospects and record what happens.
12. **Review and improve.** Track conversion rates, sales activity, revenue, and customer feedback. Update your process as you learn.

**Before considering your process ready, ask yourself:**

- Can a potential customer quickly understand what we sell and why it matters?
- Do we know which customers to prioritize?
- Have we identified the people involved in purchasing decisions?
- Are our sales goals realistic given our operating capacity?
- Do we have the materials needed to support sales conversations?
- Does every opportunity have a clear next step?
- Does someone own each stage of the process?
- Are we consistently recording information in our CRM?
- Could another team member follow our documented sales process?
- Do we know which metrics will help us identify problems and opportunities?
- Have we established a schedule for reviewing and improving the process?

Consider reviewing your sales SOP and supporting materials with your TechAlliance advisor before expanding your outreach efforts or hiring sales staff.

**Remember:** Your first sales process doesn't need to be perfect.

Start with a clear approach, execute it consistently, measure what happens, and improve it based on evidence.

The goal is to build a sales system that your business can manage, repeat, and eventually grow.
