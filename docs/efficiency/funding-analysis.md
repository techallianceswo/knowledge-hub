
# Funding analysis

## Choose the right capital to support your business goals

You've researched your market, validated your solution, developed your sales process, planned your operations, and built a cash flow forecast.

Now it's time to bring that information together and decide how your business will finance its next stage.

**Funding analysis** is the process of evaluating how much capital your company needs, when it needs it, what the money will accomplish, and which financing options best support your goals.

For some businesses, the right approach may be to grow through customer revenue and reinvest profits.

Others may need loans, grants, investors, or a combination of funding sources to develop products, expand operations, or enter new markets.

The right choice depends on your business model, growth ambitions, financial position, risk tolerance, and long-term plans.

Your funding analysis should help you answer:

- How much money does the business need?
- What will the money be used for?
- When will it be needed?
- How long will it support operations and growth?
- What milestones should the funding help us achieve?
- Which funding sources are realistic for our company?
- What are the costs, risks, and obligations of each option?
- How will today's financing decisions affect our future opportunities?

**The goal isn't to raise as much money as possible. It's to secure the resources your business needs under terms that support its long-term success.**

## Build your funding analysis on everything you've learned

Your funding strategy shouldn't be developed in isolation.

Every stage of your business development journey provides information that can help you determine whether funding is necessary and how it should be used.

### Idea: Understand the opportunity

Your early research helps explain:

- The problem you're addressing
- The potential market
- Your customer segments
- The competitive landscape
- Your initial business model
- The opportunity you're pursuing

This information helps establish why your business exists and what potential it may have.

### Discovery: Understand the customer and your approach

Your customer discovery work, expert interviews, product roadmap, and GTM planning help you explain:

- Which customer problems you've investigated
- What you've learned from potential customers
- Which assumptions have been supported or challenged
- How your proposed solution addresses the problem
- How you intend to reach customers
- What remains uncertain

This information can help you explain your strategy and the evidence behind it.

### Validation: Demonstrate customer value

Your MVP, pilot, solution interviews, and testing results provide evidence about:

- Whether customers can use your offering
- Whether it delivers meaningful value
- How customers respond to pricing
- What adoption and engagement look like
- Which improvements are still required
- What you've learned about commercial potential

Actual customer behaviour is generally stronger evidence than unsupported projections.

Be clear about what has been demonstrated and what still needs testing.

### Efficiency: Understand what growth will require

Your sales process, Ops & HR plan, and cash flow forecast help you estimate:

- Expected sales and customer acquisition
- Current operating capacity
- Hiring and resource requirements
- Recurring expenses
- Planned investments
- Cash runway
- Potential funding gaps
- The financial implications of growth

These plans should form the foundation of your funding requirements.

**Your funding analysis connects the opportunity you've identified, the evidence you've gathered, and the business you're preparing to build.**

## Determine whether you need external funding

Before exploring funding sources, consider whether your company actually needs outside capital.

External financing can help you grow, but it also introduces obligations, costs, or changes to ownership.

Ask yourself:

- Can we finance our next steps through existing cash and customer revenue?
- Could we reduce or delay some planned expenses?
- Could customer deposits, advance payments, or recurring revenue improve our cash position?
- Would slower growth allow us to maintain greater financial independence?
- Are there investments we need to make before we can generate additional revenue?
- What opportunities might we miss if we don't secure funding?
- What risks would external financing introduce?

For example, a service business with modest startup costs and paying customers may be able to grow without investors.

A company developing capital-intensive technology may need substantial funding before it can begin generating meaningful revenue.

Neither approach is automatically better.

**Funding should support a clear business need or opportunity, not become a goal on its own.**

## Calculate how much funding you need

Start with your cash flow forecast.

Identify the periods when your business may not have enough cash to cover planned expenses and maintain an appropriate operating buffer.

Consider three components.

### 1. Operating requirements

How much cash will you need to keep the business running?

This may include:

- Payroll and contractor payments
- Software and infrastructure
- Rent and utilities
- Customer support
- Materials and supplies
- Insurance
- Professional services
- Other recurring expenses

### 2. Planned investments

What additional spending is required to achieve your goals?

Examples include:

- Hiring employees
- Developing new product capabilities
- Purchasing equipment
- Expanding production
- Entering new markets
- Building sales and marketing capacity
- Meeting regulatory or technical requirements

Connect each major investment to a specific business objective.

### 3. Operating buffer

How much additional cash should you maintain to manage uncertainty?

Your buffer should reflect the risks and cash requirements of your business.

For example, a company with unpredictable sales and long customer payment cycles may need a larger buffer than a business with stable recurring revenue.

Avoid using a universal buffer percentage without considering your circumstances.

### Estimate your funding requirement

A practical approach is to:

1. Start with your current cash balance.
2. Project future cash inflows and outflows.
3. Identify the lowest projected cash balance.
4. Determine the minimum cash reserve your business should maintain.
5. Calculate the additional capital required to avoid falling below that reserve.
6. Consider financing costs, transaction expenses, and the timing of funding.

For example, imagine your forecast shows that your cash balance will fall to negative $40,000 before customer revenue begins covering expenses.

If you want to maintain a $20,000 operating reserve, your preliminary funding requirement would be $60,000.

You would then need to consider any financing costs, timing differences, and changes to your operating assumptions.

The result should be a reasoned estimate, not simply an amount that sounds attractive to investors or lenders.

## Connect funding to future milestones

Funders will often want to understand what their money will help your company accomplish.

Even if you're financing the business yourself, this is a useful planning exercise.

Define the milestones you want to reach with the available capital.

Examples include:

- Completing an essential product development phase
- Reaching a defined number of paying customers
- Establishing a repeatable sales process
- Increasing production capacity
- Hiring critical team members
- Reaching a revenue target
- Improving gross margins
- Achieving positive operating cash flow

Choose milestones that reflect meaningful business progress.

For each milestone, estimate:

- What needs to happen
- The resources required
- The expected cost
- The anticipated timeframe
- How you'll measure completion
- What happens if the milestone takes longer than expected

Your funding plan should explain how the capital supports these activities.

**A strong funding strategy connects money to measurable progress, rather than treating financing as an end in itself.**

## Understand the main funding methods

Startups can access capital in several ways.

Some options require repayment. Others involve giving investors an ownership interest in the company. Some provide funding without either obligation, although eligibility and other conditions may apply.

### Bootstrapping and customer-funded growth

**Bootstrapping** means building and growing your business using founder resources, customer revenue, and cash generated by operations rather than relying primarily on external investors.

Potential advantages include:

- Maintaining ownership and decision-making control
- Avoiding investor expectations
- Reducing dependence on future fundraising
- Encouraging disciplined spending

Potential limitations include:

- Slower growth
- Limited resources for major investments
- Greater pressure on personal or business cash
- Difficulty financing capital-intensive activities

Customer deposits, advance payments, subscriptions, and reinvested operating cash may help fund growth.

However, make sure your company can fulfil the obligations associated with advance payments.

Bootstrapping can be an intentional long-term strategy, not simply a temporary step before raising investment.

### Friends and family

Some founders raise initial capital from people in their personal networks.

This may take the form of:

- A personal gift
- A loan
- An equity investment
- A convertible investment agreement

The terms matter more than the relationship.

A loan must be evaluated differently from an equity investment, and a gift may have different tax or legal implications.

Potential advantages include access to capital from people who already know and trust the founder.

However, financial losses, misunderstandings, or unmet expectations can damage personal relationships.

If you're considering this approach:

- Explain the risks clearly.
- Don't promise returns you cannot support.
- Document the terms.
- Clarify whether the money is a gift, loan, or investment.
- Discuss repayment or ownership expectations.
- Ensure everyone understands that the business may fail.
- Obtain appropriate legal and tax advice.

Friends-and-family funding isn't available to every founder, and it isn't a prerequisite for building a successful business.

### Grants and non-repayable contributions

Grants and other contribution programs may provide funding for eligible activities without requiring founders to give up ownership.

Depending on the program, funding may support:

- Research and development
- Product innovation
- Hiring and training
- Export development
- Equipment
- Commercialization
- Other defined business activities

However, not all government or institutional funding is non-repayable.

Some programs provide repayable contributions or impose other financial obligations.

Grants may also involve:

- Eligibility restrictions
- Application deadlines
- Matching-funding requirements
- Eligible expense rules
- Reporting obligations
- Reimbursement after expenses are incurred
- Project milestones

**A grant award doesn't necessarily mean the money is immediately available.**

Review payment timing carefully and reflect it in your cash flow forecast.

Avoid building a business plan that depends entirely on funding you haven't secured.

### Debt financing

**Debt financing** involves borrowing money that must generally be repaid according to agreed terms.

Examples include:

- Business loans
- Lines of credit
- Equipment financing
- Working capital loans
- Government-backed lending programs
- Venture debt

Debt may be appropriate when your company has a reasonably predictable ability to make repayments.

Potential advantages include:

- Retaining ownership
- Defined repayment terms
- Access to capital without selling shares
- Financing assets or working capital needs

Potential limitations include:

- Interest and financing costs
- Required repayments regardless of sales performance
- Collateral or security requirements
- Personal guarantees in some circumstances
- Financial covenants or other restrictions

For example, equipment financing may make sense when a company needs machinery that will support predictable production and revenue.

Borrowing to cover ongoing losses without a credible repayment plan may create significant financial risk.

Evaluate the total borrowing cost, repayment schedule, security requirements, and impact on cash flow before proceeding.

### Equity financing

**Equity financing** involves raising capital in exchange for an ownership interest in the company.

Investors may include:

- Angel investors
- Venture capital firms
- Strategic or corporate investors
- Other private investors
- Investors participating through eligible equity crowdfunding platforms

Unlike conventional debt, equity financing generally doesn't require scheduled principal and interest payments.

However, founders give up part of the company's ownership and may accept additional governance or investor rights.

Potential advantages include:

- Access to capital without conventional loan repayments
- Ability to finance higher-risk growth initiatives
- Potential access to investor expertise and networks
- Support for longer-term growth strategies

Potential limitations include:

- Ownership dilution
- Changes to governance and decision-making
- Investor reporting requirements
- Expectations for growth and future returns
- Legal and transaction costs

Not every company is a good fit for venture capital.

VC investors generally seek businesses with the potential for substantial growth and investment returns.

A profitable company that intends to grow steadily may be better suited to another financing approach.

### Crowdfunding

Crowdfunding allows a business to raise money from multiple contributors or investors, often through an online platform.

Common models include:

**Rewards-based crowdfunding**

Supporters contribute money in exchange for a product, reward, or other benefit.

**Equity crowdfunding**

Eligible investors provide capital in exchange for securities or an ownership interest, subject to applicable securities regulations.

**Donation-based crowdfunding**

Contributors provide money without expecting a financial return or commercial reward.

Crowdfunding may help businesses test demand, finance development, or build awareness.

However, it can involve platform fees, campaign expenses, fulfilment obligations, regulatory requirements, and reputational risks.

A successful rewards campaign also creates a responsibility to deliver what was promised.

### Other funding approaches

Depending on your business model and stage, additional options may include:

- **Strategic partnerships:** Commercial arrangements in which another organization contributes capital, resources, distribution, or other support.
- **Revenue-based financing:** Capital provided in exchange for payments tied to future revenue, subject to the agreement's terms.
- **Invoice financing:** Financing secured against or generated from outstanding customer invoices.
- **Purchase-order financing:** Funding tied to fulfilling eligible customer orders.
- **Accelerators and incubators:** Programs that may provide funding, mentorship, services, or other support, sometimes in exchange for equity.

Each arrangement has its own eligibility requirements, costs, obligations, and risks.

Evaluate the specific terms rather than assuming an entire category works the same way.

## Understand common investment instruments

A funding source describes **where the money comes from**.

An investment instrument describes **how the financing arrangement is structured**.

For example, an angel investor may invest through a direct equity purchase, a SAFE, or a convertible note.

Understanding the difference is important.

### Priced equity investment

In a priced equity round, investors purchase shares based on a negotiated company valuation.

Two important concepts are:

**Pre-money valuation:** The agreed value of the company before the new investment.

**Post-money valuation:** The value immediately after the investment, generally calculated as the pre-money valuation plus the new capital invested.

For example:

A company raises $250,000 at a $1 million pre-money valuation.

Its post-money valuation would be $1.25 million.

The new investor would generally own 20% immediately after the financing, assuming a straightforward share issuance without other adjustments.

In practice, existing options, convertible instruments, and negotiated terms may affect the final ownership calculation.

Priced rounds may also involve different classes of shares and investor rights.

### Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE)

A **SAFE** is an agreement in which an investor provides capital today in exchange for the right to receive equity if specified future events occur.

SAFEs are commonly used in early-stage startup financing.

Unlike a conventional loan, a typical SAFE doesn't accrue interest or have a maturity date.

The company generally doesn't issue shares to the investor immediately.

Instead, the agreement defines how the investment may convert into equity later.

Common terms include:

**Valuation cap**

A contractual valuation threshold used when calculating the investor's conversion price.

**Discount**

A provision that may allow the investor to convert at a lower price than investors in a future financing round.

**Pre-money or post-money structure**

Different approaches to calculating how the SAFE affects ownership and dilution.

**Conversion events**

Events, such as certain future financing transactions, that may trigger conversion.

SAFEs can simplify early fundraising, but they aren't risk-free or automatically founder-friendly.

Issuing multiple SAFEs can create significant future dilution.

Different SAFE versions and terms can also produce different ownership outcomes.

**Before signing a SAFE, model how it could affect your ownership under several future financing scenarios.**

Have a qualified lawyer review the agreement, including its suitability for your jurisdiction.

### Convertible notes

A **convertible note** is a debt instrument that may convert into company equity when specified conditions are met.

Unlike a typical SAFE, convertible notes generally include:

- A principal loan amount
- An interest rate
- A maturity date
- Conversion provisions
- Potential valuation caps or discounts

For example, an investor might lend money to a startup with the expectation that the loan will convert into shares during a future equity financing.

If the relevant conversion event doesn't occur, the agreement may require repayment, renegotiation, or another outcome.

The specific terms determine what happens.

Convertible notes can provide flexibility, but founders should understand both the debt obligations and potential future dilution.

### Venture debt

**Venture debt** is a specialized form of lending generally intended for certain venture-backed companies.

It may be used to extend runway or finance growth without immediately raising another equity round.

Unlike conventional business lending, venture debt may include warrants that give the lender the right to acquire shares under specified conditions.

It also involves repayment obligations and may include financial covenants.

Venture debt isn't a general substitute for equity funding and may not be available or suitable for companies without institutional investment backing.

## Compare funding options before choosing

Each financing method has different implications.

| Funding method | Repayment obligation | Potential ownership dilution | Main consideration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bootstrapping | No external financing repayment | No | Growth limited by available resources |
| Customer-funded growth | Customer delivery obligations | No | Must fulfil customer commitments |
| Friends and family | Depends on arrangement | Depends on arrangement | Financial and personal relationship risks |
| Grants | Depends on program | Usually no | Eligibility, restrictions, and payment timing |
| Conventional debt | Yes | Usually no | Repayment capacity and borrowing terms |
| Priced equity | No scheduled loan repayment | Yes | Ownership, control, and investor expectations |
| SAFE | Typically no loan repayment | Potential future dilution | Conversion terms and future ownership |
| Convertible note | Debt terms apply | Potential future dilution | Interest, maturity, and conversion terms |
| Rewards crowdfunding | Fulfilment obligations | No | Campaign costs and delivery commitments |
| Equity crowdfunding | No scheduled loan repayment | Yes | Securities compliance and investor management |

This is a simplified comparison.

Actual obligations depend on the agreements, program requirements, and applicable laws.

When comparing options, consider:

- Total cost of capital
- Impact on cash flow
- Ownership dilution
- Decision-making control
- Security or personal guarantees
- Investor or lender expectations
- Eligibility and availability
- Time required to secure funding
- Reporting and compliance obligations
- Flexibility for future financing

The cheapest-looking option isn't always the most suitable.

For example, a low-interest loan may be inappropriate if your business cannot reliably make the repayments.

Similarly, an equity investment may provide more financial flexibility today but involve substantial long-term ownership costs.

## Understand dilution and future ownership

If you're considering equity financing, SAFEs, or convertible notes, you need to understand how future transactions may affect ownership.

**Dilution** occurs when additional shares are issued and existing shareholders own a smaller percentage of the company.

For example, if you currently own 100% of your company and issue shares representing 20% of the company after a financing round, your ownership would generally fall to 80%, assuming no other changes.

Future financing rounds, employee stock options, and convertible instruments may cause additional dilution.

However, a smaller percentage of a more valuable company may still represent a positive outcome.

The important question is whether the financing creates enough value to justify the ownership and other rights being exchanged.

Before accepting investment:

- Review your current ownership structure.
- Understand any existing convertible agreements.
- Model the proposed financing.
- Consider potential future rounds.
- Review investor rights and governance terms.
- Understand how employee equity plans may affect ownership.

Maintain an accurate **capitalization table (cap table)** showing who owns shares and other relevant equity interests.

Don't evaluate an investment offer based only on the amount of money being provided.

## Consider how funding decisions affect future growth

Financing decisions can influence your business long after the money has been spent.

For example:

A large equity round may allow you to hire quickly, but it may also create expectations for rapid growth and future fundraising.

A loan may preserve ownership, but its repayments could limit your ability to invest in product development.

A grant may reduce your funding requirements, but eligible expenses or reimbursement schedules could constrain how and when the money is used.

A SAFE may provide capital without immediate debt repayments, but future conversion could affect your ownership more than expected.

Consider how each option fits your intended business trajectory.

Ask yourself:

- What kind of company are we trying to build?
- How quickly do we want or need to grow?
- What risks are we prepared to accept?
- How much ownership and control do we want to retain?
- Will this financing create obligations that affect future decisions?
- Could this arrangement make future financing more difficult?
- What happens if we don't reach our planned milestones?
- What would a successful outcome look like for both the founders and funders?

**A good funding decision supports the business you want to build, not just the expenses you need to pay today.**

## Prepare for funding conversations

If you decide to pursue external financing, organize the information potential lenders, investors, or funding programs may request.

The requirements will vary.

For example, a lender may focus on repayment capacity, financial history, security, and cash flow.

An equity investor may place greater emphasis on market opportunity, customer traction, growth potential, team capabilities, and future returns.

Your preparation may include:

- Business overview and value proposition
- Market and competition analysis
- Customer segment research
- Product or service description
- Customer validation evidence
- Sales and GTM strategy
- Current traction and performance
- Operations and hiring plan
- Cash flow forecast
- Funding requirement and intended use
- Growth milestones
- Financial statements, where available
- Ownership and capitalization information
- Relevant legal and corporate records

Use the information you've already developed rather than creating disconnected versions of your business plan for every conversation.

Keep your assumptions, figures, and claims consistent.

If you haven't yet established an organized company data room, this is a good time to review your document management practices.

Share sensitive information selectively and use appropriate access controls.

## Put it into practice

Develop a funding strategy that supports your company's next stage of growth.

**By the end of this exercise, you should understand whether you need external funding, how much you may need, what it should accomplish, and which financing approaches are worth exploring.**

Start by reviewing your work from the Idea, Discovery, Validation, and Efficiency stages.

Then:

1. **Review your business goals.** Define what you want the company to accomplish over the next 12 to 24 months.
2. **Review your cash flow forecast.** Identify projected cash shortages, major investments, and the timing of potential funding needs.
3. **Challenge the need for external funding.** Consider whether customer revenue, expense adjustments, or slower growth could support your objectives.
4. **Calculate your funding requirement.** Estimate the capital needed to cover planned activities and maintain an appropriate operating buffer.
5. **Define the use of funds.** Explain how the money will be allocated and what each major expense is intended to achieve.
6. **Identify business milestones.** Connect the funding to measurable progress in product development, sales, operations, or financial performance.
7. **Research funding options.** Explore bootstrapping, grants, loans, equity investment, SAFEs, convertible notes, and other relevant approaches.
8. **Compare the trade-offs.** Evaluate repayment obligations, ownership dilution, financing costs, restrictions, timing, and fit with your goals.
9. **Model different scenarios.** Consider what happens if funding is delayed, the amount raised is lower than expected, or business performance differs from your forecast.
10. **Prepare supporting information.** Organize your business plans, customer evidence, financial projections, ownership records, and other relevant documents.
11. **Identify your preferred approach.** Select the funding options that best match your business needs and circumstances.
12. **Define your next steps.** Establish a timeline for researching programs, meeting potential funders, preparing applications, or seeking professional advice.

**Before moving forward, ask yourself:**

- Do we actually need external funding to achieve our goals?
- How much capital do we need, and when?
- What evidence supports our funding requirement?
- What specific milestones will the funding help us reach?
- Can our company support the repayment obligations of debt?
- Are we comfortable with the ownership dilution associated with equity financing?
- Do we understand the implications of SAFEs and convertible notes?
- Are there grants or other funding programs relevant to our activities?
- Have we considered how funding decisions may affect future financing?
- What happens if the expected funding doesn't arrive?
- Does our strategy support the kind of company we want to build?
- Have we identified where legal, accounting, or financial advice is needed?

Consider discussing your analysis with your TechAlliance advisor before committing to a financing strategy.

For significant debt, equity, SAFE, or convertible note transactions, seek advice from qualified legal and financial professionals.

### Prepare your funding analysis summary

Bring your findings together in a short document that includes:

**1. Business objectives**

What the company intends to accomplish over the next planning period.

**2. Financial position**

Current cash, forecast expenses, expected revenue, and projected runway.

**3. Funding requirement**

How much capital is needed and when it must be available.

**4. Use of funds**

How the capital will support operations, investments, and growth.

**5. Funding options**

The approaches considered and their main advantages, limitations, and risks.

**6. Preferred financing strategy**

The option or combination of options that appears most appropriate and why.

**7. Milestones and expected outcomes**

What the company intends to achieve with the funding.

**8. Risks and alternative scenarios**

What could change the plan and how the business might respond.

**9. Next steps**

The research, applications, conversations, and professional advice required before proceeding.

**Remember:** Funding is a means to build a stronger business, not a measure of success on its own.

The right financing strategy should reflect what you've learned about your customers, the value of your solution, your ability to generate revenue, and the resources needed to operate and grow.

Your goal is to make decisions that give your company the financial capacity, flexibility, and time it needs to thrive.
