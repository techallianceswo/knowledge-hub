# Elevator pitch

## Explain your business clearly, wherever the conversation takes you

You have an idea for a business, product, or service. But can you explain it to someone who's hearing about it for the first time?

An **elevator pitch** is a short, clear introduction to your business or idea. It helps someone quickly understand what you're working on, who it's for, and why it matters.

The name comes from the idea of explaining your business during a short elevator ride. In practice, you might use your pitch at a networking event, during a customer conversation, in a meeting with a potential investor, or when introducing yourself to someone new.

A good elevator pitch doesn't need to explain everything about your business. Its purpose is to communicate the most relevant information, create interest, and open the door to a more detailed conversation.

## One business, different pitches

**You shouldn't rely on a single elevator pitch for every situation.**

Different audiences care about different things. A potential customer wants to understand how your solution could help them. An investor may be more interested in your market opportunity, business model, and growth potential.

Your core message should remain consistent, but what you emphasize should change depending on who you're speaking with and what you hope to achieve.

Consider preparing different versions for these situations:

- **General introduction:** Explain what your business does, who it serves, and the problem you're working to solve. Keep it simple enough for someone unfamiliar with your industry.
- **Potential customer:** Focus on the customer's problem, how your solution addresses it, and the value it could provide. Your goal may be to understand their needs or arrange a follow-up conversation.
- **Potential investor:** Highlight the market opportunity, your proposed solution, business model, and any evidence of traction you have. Explain what you're looking for and why the opportunity may be worth exploring.
- **Potential partner:** Explain what your business does, how your work relates to theirs, and where a collaboration might create value.
- **Potential team member:** Communicate the problem you're working on, your vision for the business, and why someone might want to contribute.

You may also need shorter or longer versions depending on the situation.

For example, a brief introduction at a networking event might take 15 to 30 seconds, while a conversation with an interested investor or partner may allow more time.

The goal is to have a clear message you can adapt naturally, rather than memorizing a script for every possible situation.

## What makes an effective elevator pitch?

Regardless of your audience, a strong elevator pitch should be:

- **Clear:** Use language your listener can understand, even if they're unfamiliar with your industry.
- **Concise:** Focus on the information that matters most. Save technical details and background information for follow-up questions.
- **Relevant:** Connect your message to the interests, challenges, or goals of the person you're speaking with.
- **Specific:** Explain the problem and your proposed solution using concrete details rather than broad claims.
- **Credible:** Support your message with real information. Don't exaggerate your progress, results, or capabilities.
- **Action-oriented:** Know what you'd like to happen next, whether that's getting feedback, arranging a meeting, or continuing the conversation.

Avoid relying on buzzwords, technical jargon, or claims that are difficult to support.

If your business is still at the idea stage, be clear about what you're exploring rather than presenting assumptions as proven facts.

## Business tools

### [Founder Institute elevator pitch framework](https://fi.co/elevator-pitch)

The Founder Institute provides a simple framework for organizing a startup elevator pitch around four questions:

1. **What are you building?** Explain your business or idea and the problem it aims to solve.
2. **Why now?** Describe why the opportunity is relevant and what makes the timing important.
3. **Why you?** Explain what makes you or your team suited to pursue the opportunity.
4. **What's your ask?** Clarify what you're looking for from the conversation.

This framework can be particularly useful when introducing your business to investors, potential partners, or others interested in the opportunity behind your idea.

You don't need to include every element in every conversation. Adapt the framework to your audience and the time available.

### Audience-specific pitch framework

Use this simple exercise to develop different versions of your pitch.

Before writing, answer four questions:

1. **Who am I speaking with?** Identify your audience and what they may care about.
2. **What do they need to understand?** Choose the most relevant information about your business.
3. **Why should it matter to them?** Connect your idea or solution to their interests or needs.
4. **What do I want to happen next?** Identify a reasonable next step for the conversation.

Then build your pitch around those answers.

For example:

**General introduction**

"We're developing [product or service] to help [target audience] address [specific problem]. Our approach focuses on [main benefit or difference]."

**Potential customer**

"We're working with [target audience] who experience [specific problem]. Our proposed solution helps them [desired outcome]. I'd be interested in learning how you're currently handling this challenge."

**Potential investor**

"We're building [solution] for [target market] to address [problem]. We see an opportunity because [market insight or evidence]. We've achieved [verified progress, if available], and we're currently looking for [specific next step]."

These are starting points, not scripts you need to follow word for word.

Adjust the language to reflect your business, your current stage, and the person you're speaking with.

### Pitch feedback and refinement

Your first version probably won't be your final version.

Practise explaining your business to people with different levels of familiarity with your industry.

After each conversation, consider:

- Did the person understand what my business does?
- Which parts of my explanation needed clarification?
- What questions did they ask?
- Did I focus on information relevant to them?
- Was the next step clear?

Use their reactions and questions to improve your message.

A pitch should evolve as you learn more about your customers, refine your solution, and gather evidence about your business opportunity.

Google Docs is a collaborative writing tool you can use to draft, organize, and refine different versions of your elevator pitch.

Consider creating a single document with sections for general introductions, customer conversations, investor discussions, and partnership opportunities.

Review the platform's current features and privacy settings before recording or sharing sensitive business information.

## Put it into practice

Create three versions of your elevator pitch for different situations.

**Start by writing a general introduction to your business.**

Try to explain your idea in approximately 30 seconds, covering:

- Who you are and what you're working on
- Who your business aims to serve
- What problem you're trying to solve
- How your proposed solution addresses that problem

Once you have a clear general introduction, adapt it for two additional audiences.

For example:

1. **General introduction:** Prepare a version you could use when meeting someone at a networking event.
2. **Potential customer:** Prepare a version that emphasizes the customer's problem and opens the door to learning more about their needs.
3. **Potential investor:** Prepare a version that highlights the business opportunity, your approach, and what you're hoping to achieve.

You can choose other audiences if they're more relevant to your current goals.

**Next, practise your pitches.**

Say each version out loud and check whether it sounds natural. Try recording yourself or presenting it to someone unfamiliar with your business.

Pay attention to:

- **Clarity:** Can someone understand your idea without additional explanation?
- **Length:** Can you communicate the essential information without rushing?
- **Relevance:** Does each version focus on what matters to its intended audience?
- **Confidence:** Can you explain your idea naturally without reading a script?
- **Next steps:** Does your pitch make it easy to continue the conversation?

Ask for feedback, make adjustments, and practise again.

**Before considering your pitches ready, ask yourself:**

- Can I explain my business to someone with no technical background?
- Do I know which details matter most to customers, investors, and partners?
- Am I making claims I can support with evidence?
- Can I adjust my pitch when someone asks an unexpected question?
- Do I know what I want to achieve from each conversation?

Your elevator pitch will change as your business develops. Keep your different versions accessible, revisit them as you learn, and update them when your business direction or priorities change.
