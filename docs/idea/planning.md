
# Business Planning Tools

## Is it worth it?

Your business might still be an idea. You may not have customers, investors, or employees yet. But taking time to plan can help you understand what you're building, why it matters, and what you need to do next.

A business plan helps you organize your priorities, identify gaps in your knowledge, and determine where you may need additional support. It also gives you a starting point for testing assumptions, exploring opportunities, and making informed decisions as your idea develops.

Planning becomes especially important when seeking funding. For example, banks and other lenders may request a business plan to better understand your goals, financial projections, and how you intend to achieve them.

Your plan can also guide decisions across your business, from the processes you establish to the people you hire. Consider two manufacturers: one aims to offer the lowest prices in its market, while the other focuses on delivering premium quality. Their priorities will influence everything from equipment purchases to staffing and production processes.

**The good news is that you don't need a lengthy, traditional business plan to get started.**

For early-stage technology ventures, simpler planning tools can help you explore your business model without spending weeks preparing a detailed document.

One widely used approach is the [Business Model Canvas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_model_canvas), developed by Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur. It provides a visual framework for exploring how a business creates, delivers, and captures value.

Your initial plan doesn't need to be perfect or permanent. It will likely change as you learn more about your customers, test your assumptions, and refine your solution.

What matters is having a clear starting point. A simple plan can help you identify potential customers, define what you intend to offer, and explore how your business might generate revenue.

Ready to explore the tools?

## Business tools

### [Business Model Canvas (BMC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Business_model_canvas)

Developed by Alexander Osterwalder and introduced in his earlier research before being popularized through the 2010 book *Business Model Generation*, co-authored with Yves Pigneur, the Business Model Canvas is a widely used tool for exploring and designing business models.

It organizes nine key elements of a business on a single page, helping you see how different parts of your business connect.

The canvas can help you explore your customers, value proposition, revenue streams, costs, and other important elements without creating a lengthy business plan.

![The BMC board](/knowledge-hub/assets/images/bmc.jpg)

### [Lean Canvas](https://ashmaurya.com/blog/what-is-lean-canvas)

Created by Ash Maurya, the Lean Canvas adapts the Business Model Canvas with a stronger focus on the challenges and uncertainties faced by early-stage businesses.

It emphasizes areas such as customer problems, proposed solutions, key metrics, and competitive advantages.

The Lean Canvas can be particularly useful when you're still exploring your idea and need to identify which assumptions to test before investing significant resources.

Like the Business Model Canvas, it provides a simple, visual way to organize your thinking and update your approach as you learn.

![The Lean Canvas board](/knowledge-hub/assets/images/lean-canvas.png)

### [Project Premortem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-mortem)

A project premortem is a planning exercise that helps you identify potential risks before they become real problems.

Imagine you're looking back at your business six months or a year from now, and things haven't gone as planned. Your venture has failed, or you've fallen significantly short of your goals.

What might have gone wrong?

List realistic reasons for that outcome, then consider what you could do today to reduce the likelihood of those problems occurring.

This exercise can help you challenge assumptions, recognize potential obstacles, and prepare for risks you might otherwise overlook.

## Tech support

### [Canvanizer](https://canvanizer.com/)

An online tool for creating and working with visual business canvases, including the Business Model Canvas and Lean Canvas.

Canvanizer offers options for organizing ideas, collaborating, and exporting your work. Check its current plans and features to find the option that meets your needs.

### [Miro](https://miro.com/)

A collaborative digital whiteboard that helps you organize ideas, map processes, and work with others.

Miro offers templates for business planning, including the Business Model Canvas, Lean Canvas, and premortem exercises. A free plan is available, with limitations depending on the features you need.

## Put it into practice

Choose the Business Model Canvas or Lean Canvas and try completing a first draft for your idea.

Focus on what you currently know, and make note of any assumptions or questions that still need answers.

You don't need to fill in every section perfectly. Use the canvas to organize your thinking and identify what you should explore next.

Consider sharing your draft with your TechAlliance advisor to discuss your assumptions and priorities.
