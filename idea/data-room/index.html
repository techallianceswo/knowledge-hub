    Data room

    Organize, protect, and preserve your business information

    As your business develops, you'll create and collect important information about your customers, products, finances, operations, and decisions.

    This information is part of your company's knowledge. Keeping it organized, secure, and accessible to the right people can help you operate more effectively, make informed decisions, and prepare for future opportunities.

    A data room is a structured, access-controlled collection of business documents and information. It provides a central place to organize important records and share them with authorized people when needed.

    While data rooms are commonly associated with fundraising and investor due diligence, the same principles can help you manage your company's knowledge throughout its development.

    You don't need to wait until your business is large or preparing for investment to start organizing its information (and, in fact, it's highly recommended that you don't wait!).

    Why does it matter?

    As your company grows, important knowledge can become scattered across personal computers, email accounts, messaging platforms, and individual team members.

    This creates risks. Documents may be lost, information may become outdated, and important decisions or processes may be difficult to understand when someone leaves the company.

    Establishing a structured approach to storing business information can help you:

    • Preserve company knowledge: Keep important information available even as team members, roles, and responsibilities change.
    • Protect sensitive information: Reduce the risk of unauthorized access to confidential business, customer, and financial records.
    • Improve collaboration: Help authorized team members find the information they need without relying on individual employees.
    • Support business continuity: Maintain access to essential documents when unexpected changes or disruptions occur.
    • Prepare for opportunities: Organize the information you may need for funding applications, partnerships, audits, or investor due diligence.
    • Make informed decisions: Keep relevant records, plans, and historical information available for future reference.

    Your company's knowledge is an important business asset. Protecting and maintaining it should be part of how you operate from the beginning.

    What information should you organize?

    The information you maintain will depend on your business, industry, and stage of development.

    Consider organizing your documents into the following categories:

    • Company information: Business plans, incorporation records, ownership information, organizational structure, and strategic documents.
    • Products and technology: Product documentation, technical specifications, architecture diagrams, development roadmaps, and intellectual property records.
    • Market and customers: Market research, customer insights, competitive analysis, and relevant commercial information.
    • Financial information: Financial statements, budgets, projections, revenue reports, and other financial records.
    • Operations and processes: Internal procedures, workflows, policies, supplier information, and operational documentation.
    • People and team: Organizational charts, role descriptions, onboarding materials, and relevant employment documentation.
    • Legal and compliance: Contracts, agreements, licences, policies, and regulatory documentation.
    • Funding and partnerships: Pitch decks, funding applications, investor materials, partnership agreements, and due diligence documents.

    Not every category will apply to your business immediately.

    Start with the information you already have and identify which documents are essential to your operations.

    Some information, such as passwords, credentials, confidential employee records, and sensitive customer data, requires additional protection and should be managed through appropriate dedicated systems rather than ordinary shared folders.

    Keep your company's knowledge safe

    Organizing information is only part of the process. You also need to consider how it's stored, protected, maintained, and accessed.

    A shared folder alone doesn't guarantee that your information is secure.

    Consider these practices:

    • Use company-controlled accounts: Store business information in systems managed by your company rather than relying on personal accounts.
    • Control access: Give people access only to the information they need for their responsibilities.
    • Protect accounts: Use strong authentication, including multi-factor authentication (MFA), wherever available.
    • Maintain backups: Keep recoverable copies of important information and periodically test your ability to restore them.
    • Manage permissions: Review who can access, edit, download, or share documents, especially when team members or external collaborators change.
    • Keep information current: Establish a process for reviewing and updating important documents.
    • Track document changes: Use version history or document management practices to reduce confusion and preserve important revisions.
    • Protect confidential information: Apply appropriate safeguards to customer data, intellectual property, financial records, and other sensitive materials.
    • Plan for continuity: Make sure essential company knowledge remains accessible to authorized people if a team member becomes unavailable or leaves.

    For sensitive information, consider whether your storage platform provides the security controls, data protection features, and access management your business requires.

    Depending on the type of information you handle, additional legal, privacy, contractual, or regulatory requirements may apply.

    Business tools

    Knowledge management structure

    A knowledge management structure helps you decide how information should be organized, maintained, and accessed within your company.

    Start by identifying the main categories of information your business creates and uses.

    For each category, consider:

    • Purpose: Why does your business need this information?
    • Location: Where should it be stored?
    • Ownership: Who is responsible for maintaining it?
    • Access: Who should be allowed to view or modify it?
    • Maintenance: How often should it be reviewed or updated?
    • Retention: How long should it be kept, and when should it be securely archived or deleted?

    This approach helps establish clear responsibilities and reduces the risk of important knowledge becoming disorganized or dependent on one person.

    Document management checklist

    A document management checklist can help you assess whether your company's important records are organized and protected.

    For each document or group of documents, track:

    • Document name and category
    • Storage location
    • Responsible team member
    • Last review or update
    • Access permissions
    • Confidentiality or sensitivity level
    • Backup and recovery arrangements

    Use the checklist to identify missing documents, outdated information, unnecessary access permissions, or other areas that need attention.

    Investor and partner data room

    When preparing for fundraising, partnerships, or due diligence, you may need to share selected company information with external parties.

    Instead of granting access to your entire internal document library, create a separate data room containing only the information relevant to that specific request.

    For example, an investor data room may include:

    • Company overview and pitch deck
    • Business model and market analysis
    • Product information and development roadmap
    • Financial statements and projections
    • Customer traction and business metrics
    • Ownership structure and capitalization table
    • Relevant contracts and legal documents
    • Funding requirements and proposed use of funds

    The documents required will depend on the purpose and stage of the discussion.

    Keep your internal knowledge repository separate from external data rooms. This allows you to maintain your company's information while controlling what you share with investors, partners, or other third parties.

    Review confidentiality requirements and access permissions before sharing sensitive documents.

    Tech support

    Google Drive

    Google Drive is a cloud storage and file-sharing platform that can help you organize documents, collaborate with team members, and manage access to business information.

    You can create folders, share documents, and maintain version history for supported files.

    For company knowledge management, consider using an appropriate Google Workspace setup with organization-managed accounts and shared storage rather than relying on an individual's personal account.

    Review your storage, sharing, and security settings before using it for sensitive business information.

    Microsoft OneDrive

    Microsoft OneDrive provides cloud storage and file-sharing capabilities, with integration into Microsoft 365 applications such as Word, Excel, and PowerPoint.

    It can help team members store and work with business documents while maintaining access across devices.

    For company-wide knowledge, consider how OneDrive fits into your organization's broader document management approach, including shared ownership, access permissions, and employee transitions.

    Microsoft SharePoint

    Microsoft SharePoint is a document management and collaboration platform designed to help organizations organize and share information.

    It supports document libraries, permissions, version history, and collaboration across teams.

    SharePoint can be useful for businesses that need structured, company-managed document storage with multiple contributors and different access requirements.

    Its available features and security controls depend on your Microsoft 365 configuration and subscription.

    Put it into practice

    Create a basic, company-controlled document repository where you can organize and maintain your business information.

    Start with a simple folder structure:

    1. Company information
    2. Products and technology
    3. Market and customers
    4. Financial information
    5. Operations and processes
    6. People and team
    7. Legal and compliance
    8. Funding and partnerships

    Adjust these categories to match your business. You don't need to create every folder or prepare every document immediately.

    Once you've established your structure:

    1. Organize: Identify your existing business documents and move them into appropriate company-managed storage.
    2. Review: Check that important documents are accurate, clearly named, and up to date.
    3. Protect: Review access permissions, enable available security protections, and identify information that requires more secure storage.
    4. Assign ownership: Decide who will maintain each category of information.
    5. Plan for recovery: Confirm that important information is backed up and can be restored if needed.
    6. Maintain: Establish a simple process for reviewing and updating your company's knowledge as the business evolves.

    Before considering your initial setup complete, ask yourself:

    • Can authorized team members find the information they need?
    • Is important business knowledge stored in company-controlled systems?
    • Could the business access essential information if a team member left unexpectedly?
    • Are confidential documents protected from unnecessary access?
    • Do we have a reliable way to recover important information if it's lost or deleted?
    • Do we know who is responsible for keeping our documents current?

    You don't need a complex system to get started. Focus on creating a structure that's practical for your business and can evolve as your needs change.
