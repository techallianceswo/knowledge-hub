    Competition analysis

    Understand your competition and find opportunities in the market

    You're building on your business idea right now, but how are your potential customers solving the same problem today?

    Understanding your competition is an important part of exploring a business opportunity. It helps you learn what solutions already exist, how companies attract customers, and where your idea might offer something different.

    A competition analysis, also called a competitive analysis, is a structured way to research other businesses and alternatives in your market. It involves comparing their products, pricing, positioning, strengths, weaknesses, and customer experiences.

    The goal isn't simply to find out who's competing with you. It's to understand the choices your potential customers have and identify where there may be unmet needs or opportunities worth exploring.

    You don't need to know everything about every competitor. Start by building a useful picture of the market and focus on the information that can help you make better decisions.

    Who are your competitors?

    Your competition may extend beyond businesses offering products or services similar to yours.

    Consider three types of competition:

    • Direct competitors: Businesses offering similar solutions to the same target customers. For example, two software platforms designed to help small businesses manage their finances.
    • Indirect competitors: Businesses offering different solutions that address a similar customer need. For example, a specialized accounting platform competing with a broader business management platform.
    • Substitutes and alternatives: Other ways customers can solve the problem, including spreadsheets, manual processes, internal teams, or existing tools used for a different purpose.

    Sometimes, your biggest competitor is the way customers already do things.

    A potential customer may recognize the problem you're trying to solve but still prefer their current approach because it's familiar, affordable, or requires less effort to adopt.

    Understanding these alternatives can help you identify what your solution would need to offer to encourage customers to change.

    What should you research?

    Once you've identified potential competitors, take a closer look at how they operate and what customers value about their offerings.

    Consider researching:

    • Market presence and positioning: Where do competitors operate, who do they serve, and how do they position themselves? What is known about their market share or business size?
    • Products and services: What solutions do they offer? What features, variations, or service levels are available?
    • Pricing and revenue models: How much do they charge? Do they use subscriptions, one-time purchases, usage-based pricing, or other models?
    • Distribution channels: How do customers access their products or services? Do they sell directly, through partners, or through other channels?
    • Marketing and customer acquisition: How do they reach customers? What messages, channels, and promotional approaches do they use?
    • Value propositions and differentiators: Why might customers choose them? What makes their offerings attractive or difficult to compete with?
    • Target customers: Who appears to use their solutions? What industries, business sizes, locations, or customer groups do they serve?
    • Customer experience and reputation: What do customers appreciate or criticize? What problems remain unresolved?

    You may not find reliable information for every category.

    Private companies, for example, may not disclose their revenue, market share, or detailed customer demographics.

    Separate verified information from estimates and assumptions. If reliable data isn't available, record the gap rather than treating an unsupported estimate as fact.

    Look beyond the features

    It's easy to focus on what competing products can do. However, features are only one part of why customers choose a solution.

    Customers may also consider:

    • Price and overall cost
    • Ease of use and implementation
    • Customer support
    • Reliability and quality
    • Availability and delivery time
    • Compatibility with existing tools
    • Brand reputation and trust
    • Switching costs and contractual commitments

    For example, a competitor may offer more features than your proposed solution but require a complex setup process.

    That could represent an opportunity for a simpler alternative, particularly if your target customers value ease of use.

    However, an apparent gap doesn't automatically mean customers want a new solution.

    Use your competition analysis to identify questions and assumptions you can investigate further during customer discovery.

    Business tools

    Competitor comparison matrix

    A competitor comparison matrix helps you organize information about different companies in a consistent format.

    Start by selecting a manageable group of competitors and alternatives. Include direct competitors as well as other solutions your customers might consider.

    Create a table comparing the factors most relevant to your market.

    Criteria Your idea Competitor A Competitor B
    Target customers
    Main problem addressed
    Products or services
    Pricing model
    Key features
    Distribution channels
    Main differentiators
    Customer feedback

    Add or remove criteria based on your business.

    For your own idea, distinguish between what you're proposing and what you've already tested or demonstrated.

    Use the completed matrix to identify patterns, differences, and questions that deserve further investigation.

    Perceptual map

    A perceptual map, also called a positioning map, is a visual tool that helps you compare how different solutions are positioned or perceived in the market.

    It typically uses two axes representing characteristics that matter to customers.

    For example, you could compare:

    • Price versus perceived quality
    • Ease of use versus available features
    • Level of customization versus implementation complexity
    • Customer support versus cost

    Place each competitor on the map according to its position on those two characteristics.

    You can also include your proposed solution to visualize where you intend to compete.

    A perceptual map can help you identify crowded areas of the market, possible positioning opportunities, and differences between competitors.

    Choose characteristics that matter to your target customers. A map based on irrelevant factors may look useful without providing meaningful insight.

    Whenever possible, use customer feedback, research, or measurable information to support where you place each competitor.

    If you're relying on your own judgement, label the map as an initial estimate that still needs validation.

    Competitive strengths and weaknesses analysis

    Once you've gathered information, review what each competitor appears to do well and where customers may experience difficulties.

    Consider:

    • What are their strongest competitive advantages?
    • What do customers consistently value about their solutions?
    • What complaints or limitations appear repeatedly?
    • Which strengths would be difficult for a new business to replicate?
    • Where might there be opportunities to serve customers differently?

    Look for patterns across several competitors rather than relying on a single review or observation.

    Use your findings to identify potential opportunities, but avoid assuming that every competitor weakness represents an unmet customer need.

    Tech support

    ChatGPT / Claude / DeepSeek / Augure[1]

    "Traditional" LLM tools can help you organize competitor research, identify potential alternatives, compare publicly available information, and summarize your findings.

    When web research is available, you can use it to investigate competitor websites, pricing, positioning, and customer feedback.

    Use the suggested research prompt below as a starting point, and review the sources behind important claims before relying on the results.

    [1]: Augure is the only fully Canadian option from this list

    Perplexity

    Perplexity is an AI-powered research and search tool that can help you explore competitors, find relevant online sources, and investigate market information.

    It can be useful for discovering companies you may not have considered and collecting initial information about their products, positioning, and public presence.

    Check the original sources, especially when reviewing financial information, market estimates, or claims about competitors.

    Suggested AI research prompt

    You can use the following prompt with an AI research tool that supports web searches.

    Replace the information in brackets with details about your business.

    Act as an experienced market research analyst specializing in competitive analysis for startups.

I'm exploring a venture called [VENTURE NAME].

Website (if available): [WEBSITE URL]
Proposed products or services: [DESCRIPTION]
Business location: [CITY, PROVINCE/STATE, COUNTRY]
Target markets: [GEOGRAPHIC MARKETS OR ONLINE]
Target customers: [CUSTOMER DESCRIPTION]
Problem we aim to solve: [CUSTOMER PROBLEM]

I need you to research our competitive landscape, including direct competitors, indirect competitors, and alternative ways customers currently solve the same problem.

Use credible, publicly available sources and provide links to support your findings.

PART 1: IDENTIFY THE COMPETITION

Create a table identifying relevant competitors and alternatives.

For each one, include:
- Company or solution name
- Website
- Headquarters or operating location, if known
- Geographic markets served
- Type of competition (direct, indirect, or substitute)
- Brief explanation of why customers might consider this alternative

PART 2: RESEARCH THE COMPETITORS

For each relevant competitor, investigate:

1. Market presence and positioning
   - Target markets and customer segments
   - Market share, revenue, or company size, where reliable data is available
   - Main positioning and reputation

2. Products and services
   - Main offerings
   - Features, variations, and service levels
   - Notable capabilities or limitations

3. Pricing and revenue model
   - Publicly available prices
   - Subscription, one-time, usage-based, or other pricing models
   - Additional products, services, or fees

4. Distribution channels
   - Direct sales, online platforms, retail, partners, or other channels
   - Relevant distribution relationships

5. Marketing and customer acquisition
   - Main marketing messages
   - Visible marketing channels and campaigns
   - Evidence of advertising, content marketing, partnerships, or other acquisition approaches

6. Value proposition and differentiators
   - Main reasons customers may choose the competitor
   - Important competitive advantages
   - Strengths that may be difficult for a new entrant to replicate

7. Customer base
   - Customer industries, company sizes, locations, or other relevant characteristics
   - Demographic information only when supported by credible evidence

8. Customer feedback and reputation
   - Common positive and negative feedback
   - Recurring complaints or unmet needs
   - Potential reasons customers remain with or switch away from the solution

PART 3: RESEARCH SOURCES

Use relevant sources, including:
- Official competitor websites
- Product documentation and pricing pages
- Industry reports and public databases
- Statistics Canada and other government sources
- News articles and company announcements
- Customer review platforms
- Public social media discussions
- Publicly available advertising and promotional materials
- Other credible sources relevant to the market

For every important finding, provide a source link.

Distinguish clearly between:
- Verified facts
- Estimates or interpretations
- Information that could not be verified

Do not invent revenue figures, market share, customer demographics, pricing, or other unavailable information.

If sources conflict, explain the discrepancy.

Consider the publication date of each source and identify information that may be outdated.

Do not treat promotional claims as independently verified facts.

PART 4: COMPETITIVE COMPARISON

Create a comparison table summarizing the most relevant competitors across:
- Target customers
- Main offerings
- Pricing
- Positioning
- Key strengths
- Key weaknesses
- Potential opportunities

PART 5: MARKET OPPORTUNITIES

Conclude with no more than two paragraphs explaining:

1. Where the market appears crowded or where competitors offer similar solutions, pricing, or positioning.
2. Where there may be opportunities to address unmet needs, improve customer experiences, or offer a different approach.

Base your conclusions on the research.

Clearly identify any opportunities that remain hypotheses requiring customer validation.

Present the results in a structured report with tables, concise explanations, and source links.

    Important: AI-generated research should be treated as a starting point, not a completed competitive analysis.

    AI tools can overlook competitors, misinterpret reviews, use outdated information, or produce unsupported claims.

    Verify important findings using original sources and, whenever possible, complement online research with conversations with potential customers.

    Avoid uploading confidential company information or personal customer data to AI tools without appropriate authorization and safeguards.

    Trustpilot / Capterra / G2

    Trustpilot, Capterra, and G2 are online review platforms where consumers share experiences with businesses.

    You can use them to explore customer feedback, identify recurring complaints, and understand what customers appreciate about competing companies.

    Look beyond overall ratings. Read individual reviews, consider their dates, look for consistent themes, and remember that online reviews may not represent the full customer base.

    Put it into practice

    Create an initial competition analysis for your business idea.

    Start by identifying five competitors or alternatives that your potential customers might consider.

    Try to include a mix of direct competitors, indirect competitors, and substitutes.

    Then:

    1. Research: Visit their websites and review their products, services, pricing, and positioning.
    2. Compare: Create a competitor comparison matrix using the factors most relevant to your market.
    3. Explore feedback: Look for customer reviews and identify recurring strengths, complaints, or unmet needs.
    4. Visualize: Build a perceptual map using two characteristics that matter to your target customers.
    5. Identify opportunities: Write down potential gaps, areas of strong competition, and assumptions you need to investigate further.

    You can use the suggested AI prompt to support your research, but make sure you verify the information that influences your conclusions.

    Before considering your initial analysis complete, ask yourself:

    • Who are my most relevant direct and indirect competitors?
    • What alternatives are potential customers already using?
    • Why do customers choose these solutions?
    • What do existing competitors do particularly well?
    • What complaints or unmet needs appear repeatedly?
    • Where might my proposed solution offer something different?
    • Which findings are supported by evidence, and which are still assumptions?

    Use your findings to prepare a short summary of the competitive landscape and the opportunities you want to investigate.

    Your analysis doesn't need to be exhaustive. It should give you a clearer understanding of the market and help you decide what to explore next.

    Consider reviewing your findings with your TechAlliance advisor to identify assumptions worth testing during the Discovery stage.

    As your business evolves, revisit your analysis to account for new competitors, changing customer needs, and developments in your market.
