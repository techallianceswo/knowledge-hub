    Potential customer segments

    Find out who might need your solution

    You have an idea for a product or service. But who would actually benefit from it, and who might be willing to pay for it?

    Understanding your potential customers is an important step in exploring a business opportunity.

    A customer segment is a group of people or organizations that share characteristics, needs, behaviours, or challenges relevant to your business.

    Different groups may experience the same problem in different ways. They may also have different budgets, priorities, purchasing habits, and expectations.

    For example, a software solution designed to improve scheduling could serve independent professionals, small businesses, and large organizations. Although these customers may share a similar challenge, their requirements and purchasing decisions could be very different.

    Rather than trying to serve everyone, start by identifying the groups most likely to benefit from your proposed solution.

    The goal is to understand where your idea might create value and which potential customers are worth learning more about.

    Understand the different types of customers

    The way you research potential customers depends partly on how your business intends to sell its products or services.

    Consider these common business models:

    • Business-to-consumer (B2C): You sell directly to individual customers. Relevant characteristics may include location, lifestyle, purchasing habits, needs, and spending priorities.
    • Business-to-business (B2B): You sell to other companies or organizations. Relevant characteristics may include industry, company size, location, operational challenges, and purchasing processes.
    • Business-to-business-to-consumer (B2B2C): You reach end customers through another business or platform. You'll need to understand both the organizations you work with and the people who ultimately use or purchase your solution.

    Some businesses serve more than one type of customer.

    For example, a technology company might sell software directly to organizations while also offering a version for individual users.

    Understanding these differences helps you decide what information to research and which potential customers to prioritize.

    What makes a useful customer segment?

    A useful customer segment is more specific than a broad category such as "small businesses" or "people who use technology."

    Consider the characteristics that could influence whether someone needs, chooses, or pays for your solution.

    For B2B customers, explore:

    • Industry or business sector
    • Company size and stage of development
    • Geographic location
    • Department or team experiencing the problem
    • Decision-makers and people involved in purchasing
    • Existing tools, processes, or alternatives
    • Budget and purchasing requirements
    • Urgency and impact of the problem

    For B2C customers, explore:

    • Geographic location
    • Relevant demographic characteristics
    • Needs, interests, and behaviours
    • Purchasing and consumption habits
    • Current solutions or alternatives
    • Spending patterns and price sensitivity
    • Frequency and urgency of the problem
    • Where they discover, evaluate, and purchase products

    Not every characteristic will matter equally.

    For example, age may be relevant for some consumer products but much less useful than purchasing behaviour or the specific problem a customer experiences.

    Focus on characteristics that help you understand customer needs and purchasing decisions.

    Research supply and demand

    Before choosing a customer segment, explore what is already available in your target market and whether there are signs of demand for your proposed solution.

    Supply refers to the products, services, and alternatives currently available to customers.

    Demand refers to the interest, need, and willingness to obtain or pay for a solution.

    Start by asking:

    • What products or services are already available in the market?
    • Who currently provides them?
    • Are people searching for solutions to the problem?
    • Are there reports or studies about relevant purchasing behaviours?
    • What alternatives are customers currently using?
    • Are there indications that some customer groups are underserved?
    • Are there geographic markets where the problem may be more relevant?

    Online search activity, industry reports, customer reviews, and competitor information can provide useful signals.

    However, search interest doesn't automatically mean purchase demand.

    People may search for information without intending to buy anything. Likewise, a problem may exist even when customers aren't searching for a specific product category.

    Use market research to identify potential opportunities, then investigate those opportunities through customer conversations and other validation activities.

    Identify your potential early adopters

    Not every customer will be equally interested in trying a new solution.

    Early adopters are customers who may be more willing to explore a new product or service because they recognize the problem and have a strong reason to address it.

    They may already be looking for alternatives, spending money on imperfect solutions, or investing significant time in workarounds.

    When considering potential early adopters, ask:

    • Who experiences the problem most frequently or intensely?
    • Who is already trying to solve it?
    • Who has the resources or authority to act?
    • Who would benefit most from a better approach?
    • Who might be willing to try an early version of the solution?

    At the Idea stage, your early adopter segment is still a hypothesis.

    You may have a strong initial guess, but you'll need customer discovery and validation to understand whether that group is genuinely interested.

    Business tools

    Customer segmentation matrix

    A customer segmentation matrix helps you compare potential customer groups and decide which ones deserve further research.

    Start by identifying three to five customer segments that might benefit from your solution.

    Then compare them using criteria relevant to your business.

    Criteria Segment A Segment B Segment C
    Customer description
    Main problem
    Current alternatives
    Problem frequency or urgency
    Potential willingness to pay
    Geographic market
    Ease of reaching customers
    Evidence of demand

    Use the matrix to identify which segments appear most promising.

    Clearly distinguish between findings supported by research and assumptions that still need validation.

    TAM, SAM, and SOM

    TAM, SAM, and SOM are market-sizing concepts that help you understand the potential scale of a business opportunity.

    • Total Addressable Market (TAM): The total market demand for a product or service if you could serve everyone within the defined market.
    • Serviceable Available Market (SAM): The portion of that market your business could realistically serve based on its offering, geography, and business model.
    • Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM): The portion of the serviceable market your business could reasonably aim to capture within a defined period.

    These estimates can help you understand whether a potential customer segment represents a meaningful opportunity.

    Avoid relying on broad industry figures without considering whether the customers included are relevant to your specific solution.

    For an early-stage venture, a smaller, clearly defined market estimate supported by reasonable assumptions may be more useful than a large figure with little connection to your target customers.

    Ideal customer profile (ICP)

    An ideal customer profile describes the characteristics of the customer or organization most likely to benefit from your solution.

    For a B2B business, an ICP might include:

    • Industry
    • Company size
    • Geographic location
    • Relevant department
    • Operational challenge
    • Existing solutions
    • Purchasing requirements

    For a B2C business, it may focus more on needs, behaviours, circumstances, and purchasing motivations.

    Your initial ICP should be treated as a working hypothesis.

    As you speak with potential customers, you can refine it based on what you learn about their problems, priorities, and willingness to act.

    Early adopter hypothesis

    Use this simple framework to describe who you believe might adopt your solution first.

    "We believe [SPECIFIC CUSTOMER SEGMENT] experiences [PROBLEM] and currently addresses it through [EXISTING SOLUTION OR WORKAROUND].

    We believe this group may be interested in a new solution because [REASON OR EVIDENCE].

    We need to investigate [MAIN ASSUMPTIONS] before deciding whether to focus on this segment."

    This statement helps turn broad market research into a clear hypothesis you can test during customer discovery.

    Tech support

    ChatGPT / Claude / DeepSeek / Augure[1]

    "Traditional" LLM tools can help you research what is available; they can also help you identify industry reports, demographic information, market trends, and potential sources of demand.

    Use the suggested research prompt below to organize your investigation.

    [1]: Augure is the only fully Canadian option from this list

    Perplexity

    Perplexity is an AI-powered research and search tool that can help you investigate customer groups, discover relevant market information, and locate supporting sources.

    It may be useful for exploring unfamiliar industries, identifying market trends, and finding public reports about purchasing behaviours or customer needs.

    Review original sources before relying on important findings.

    Suggested AI research prompt

    Use the following prompt with an AI research tool that supports web searches.

    Replace the information in brackets with details about your business.

    Act as an experienced business development and market research analyst specializing in customer segmentation for startups.

I'm exploring a venture called [VENTURE NAME].

Website (if available): [WEBSITE URL]
Proposed products or services: [DESCRIPTION]
Business location: [CITY, PROVINCE/STATE, COUNTRY]
Initial target markets: [GEOGRAPHIC MARKETS OR ONLINE]
Business model: [B2B, B2C, B2B2C, OR UNKNOWN]
Problem we aim to solve: [CUSTOMER PROBLEM]

I need you to research potential customer segments, assess available evidence of demand, and identify which groups may be worth investigating first.

Use credible, publicly available sources and provide links to support important findings.

PART 1: RESEARCH THE INITIAL MARKET

Investigate the proposed products or services within my initial target market.

Research:

1. Existing supply
   - What comparable products, services, and alternatives are available?
   - Which types of providers serve the market?
   - Are there signs of a crowded or underserved market?

2. Potential demand
   - What evidence exists of customer interest or demand?
   - Are people searching for these products or the problems they address?
   - Are there relevant purchasing, usage, or consumption trends?
   - What credible reports, statistics, or studies describe the market?

Distinguish between evidence of interest, evidence of purchasing behaviour, and evidence of willingness to pay.

Do not assume search volume alone proves demand.

PART 2: IDENTIFY POTENTIAL CUSTOMER SEGMENTS

Identify customer groups that may benefit from the proposed solution.

For B2B opportunities, investigate:
- Relevant industries or business sectors
- Company size and stage
- Geographic concentration
- Departments or teams experiencing the problem
- Potential decision-makers and purchasing stakeholders
- Main customer problems
- Existing solutions and workarounds
- Available evidence of spending or purchasing behaviour
- Potential market size

For B2C opportunities, investigate:
- Relevant demographic and behavioural characteristics
- Geographic concentration
- Customer needs and motivations
- Main customer problems
- Existing solutions and workarounds
- Available evidence of spending or purchasing behaviour
- Potential market size

For B2B2C opportunities, also investigate:
- Potential intermediary businesses or platforms
- Where end customers currently discover or purchase alternatives
- How the needs of intermediary businesses differ from those of end customers

Only include demographic characteristics that are relevant to the product or purchasing decision.

PART 3: EXPLORE ALTERNATIVE MARKETS

If evidence of demand in the initial market is limited, investigate other potential geographic markets.

Consider relevant locations within:
- Canada
- North America
- Other international markets, when appropriate

Research:
- Where people or organizations appear to experience the problem
- Where relevant products or alternatives are being searched for or purchased
- Whether competing solutions already serve those markets
- Comparable geographic markets with similar customer characteristics
- Potential barriers to entering those markets

Do not conclude that a market has no demand simply because public search or market data is limited.

Explain the evidence and limitations behind each recommendation.

PART 4: ESTIMATE MARKET SIZE

Where credible data is available, estimate the potential market size for relevant customer segments.

Consider:
- Total Addressable Market (TAM)
- Serviceable Available Market (SAM)
- Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM), when reasonable assumptions are available

Explain:
- The data sources used
- The calculation method
- Important assumptions
- Geographic and customer segment boundaries
- Limitations or uncertainty

Do not invent market size figures or present unsupported estimates as verified facts.

PART 5: IDENTIFY POTENTIAL EARLY ADOPTERS

Compare the most promising customer segments based on:
- Problem urgency or frequency
- Existing spending or effort to solve the problem
- Evidence of demand
- Potential willingness and ability to pay
- Ease of reaching the customer group
- Competitive alternatives
- Potential barriers to adoption

Recommend one segment as the strongest initial early adopter hypothesis.

Explain why this group appears promising and what evidence would be needed to validate the recommendation.

Do not present the recommendation as a confirmed customer segment.

PART 6: RESEARCH SOURCES

Use relevant and credible sources, including:
- Statistics Canada and other government databases
- Industry and market research reports
- Official economic and demographic data
- News articles and credible industry publications
- Public customer reviews and discussions
- Competitor websites and pricing information
- Google Trends and other search trend platforms
- Publicly available competitive intelligence tools
- Other relevant sources

For important findings, provide source links.

Distinguish between:
- Verified facts
- Estimates or interpretations
- Assumptions requiring validation
- Information that could not be verified

Consider the publication dates and limitations of sources.

Do not invent customer demographics, purchasing behaviour, search volume, market size, or other unavailable information.

PART 7: FINAL OUTPUT

Present:
1. A brief overview of supply and demand in the initial market
2. A comparison table of potential customer segments
3. Relevant geographic market opportunities
4. Market size estimates, where supported
5. A recommended early adopter hypothesis
6. The most important assumptions to test through customer discovery
7. Source links for important findings

Conclude with no more than two paragraphs summarizing the most promising customer segments, relevant geographic markets, and recommended early adopter hypothesis.

Keep conclusions practical, evidence-based, and clear about uncertainty.

    Important: AI-assisted market research can help you identify potential opportunities, but it cannot confirm that customers need or will purchase your solution.

    AI tools may rely on incomplete data, outdated reports, or assumptions that don't reflect your specific market.

    Verify important findings using original sources and use customer conversations to investigate whether your proposed segments experience the problem you're trying to solve.

    Avoid sharing confidential business information or personal customer data with AI tools without appropriate authorization and safeguards.

    Statistics Canada

    Statistics Canada provides official data about Canada's population, businesses, industries, economy, and other topics.

    You can use its data to investigate demographic characteristics, business populations, geographic markets, and other factors relevant to your customer segments.

    Look for datasets that closely match your target customers rather than relying only on broad national statistics.

    Google Trends

    Google Trends helps you explore relative search interest for topics and search terms across locations and time periods.

    It can be useful for comparing interest in different problems, products, or markets.

    Remember that Google Trends shows normalized search interest, not total demand or confirmed purchasing behaviour.

    Google Keyword Planner

    Google Keyword Planner can help you explore keywords associated with your product, service, or the problem you're trying to solve.

    It provides keyword ideas and search-related estimates that may help you understand how people look for relevant solutions.

    Access and available data may depend on your Google Ads account and settings.

    Use keyword information as one research signal rather than proof of customer demand.

    Put it into practice

    Identify three potential customer segments for your business idea.

    Start by describing the problem your solution aims to address.

    Then consider who might experience that problem and how their needs could differ.

    For each potential segment:

    1. Describe: Define the customer group using relevant characteristics such as industry, business size, geography, behaviours, or needs.
    2. Research: Look for credible information about the segment, including its size, relevant trends, and existing solutions.
    3. Understand alternatives: Identify how these customers currently address the problem.
    4. Explore demand: Look for evidence that the problem matters and that customers may be willing to invest time or money in solving it.
    5. Compare: Use a customer segmentation matrix to assess which groups appear most promising.

    Once you've compared your segments, select one as your initial early adopter hypothesis.

    Write a short statement explaining:

    • Who the potential early adopters are
    • What problem you believe they experience
    • How they're currently addressing it
    • Why they might be interested in a different solution
    • What you still need to learn

    Before moving forward, ask yourself:

    • Have I defined customer groups that are specific enough to research?
    • What evidence suggests these customers experience the problem?
    • Am I confusing general market interest with actual demand?
    • Do I understand how these customers currently solve the problem?
    • Which segment appears most promising, and why?
    • What assumptions still need to be tested through customer conversations?

    Your initial customer segments don't need to be perfect. They're a starting point for learning more about your market.

    Consider reviewing your findings and early adopter hypothesis with your TechAlliance advisor before moving into customer discovery.

    The next step is to speak with potential customers, understand their experiences, and investigate whether the problem you've identified is important enough for them to address.
