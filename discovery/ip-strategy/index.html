    IP strategy

    Understand your intellectual property and avoid potential risks

    As you explore your business idea and begin planning a product or service, you'll make decisions about technology, branding, designs, content, and other information that may have intellectual property implications.

    Intellectual property (IP) refers to creations of the mind that may be protected through legal rights, such as inventions, original creative works, designs, and trademarks.

    An IP strategy is a plan for identifying, managing, protecting, and using intellectual property in ways that support your business goals.

    At the Discovery stage, your priority isn't necessarily to register patents or trademarks immediately.

    Instead, focus on understanding:

    • What intellectual property your business may be creating
    • What intellectual property belongs to other people or organizations
    • Whether existing IP rights could affect your proposed product or business
    • Who owns the work being created for your venture
    • What information you may need to protect before sharing it publicly
    • When you should seek professional advice about registration or other protection

    Understanding existing IP rights is just as important as protecting your own.

    Addressing these questions early can help you avoid costly changes and make more informed decisions as your product develops.

    Understand the different types of IP

    Different types of intellectual property protect different aspects of a business.

    Some common examples include:

    • Patents: Can protect qualifying inventions, including certain products, processes, and technological improvements. Examples include:
    • A new medical device that uses an innovative mechanism to monitor a patient's health.
    • A novel manufacturing process that reduces material waste.

    • A new battery technology that improves energy storage efficiency.

    • Trademarks: Help distinguish your goods or services from those of other businesses. They can include names, logos, and other identifying signs. Examples include:

    • A distinctive company name used to identify your business's products or services.
    • A recognizable logo displayed on your products, website, or packaging.

    • A unique product name used to distinguish your offering from competitors.

    • Copyright: Can protect original works such as software code, written content, illustrations, and other creative material. Examples include:

    • Original source code written for a software application.
    • Photographs, illustrations, or graphics created for your website.

    • Original training materials, instructional videos, or written guides.

    • Industrial designs: Can protect qualifying visual features of a finished product, such as its shape, configuration, or ornamentation. Examples include:

    • The distinctive exterior shape of a wearable technology device.
    • A unique decorative pattern applied to a consumer product.

    • The original visual design of a product's casing or enclosure.

    • Trade secrets: Can protect valuable confidential business information when appropriate measures are taken to keep it secret. Examples include:

    • A confidential manufacturing formula or production method.
    • A proprietary algorithm or internal process that provides a competitive advantage and is kept confidential.
    • A confidential customer pricing model or business strategy that isn't publicly available.

    These protections work differently.

    For example, copyright generally arises automatically when an eligible original work is created, while patent protection generally requires an application and examination process.

    Trade secrets depend on maintaining confidentiality rather than registering the information.

    An idea by itself isn't automatically protected simply because you thought of it first.

    Understanding these distinctions can help you recognize which aspects of your business may need attention.

    Make sure you're not infringing on someone else's IP

    Before investing significant resources into developing a product, choosing a brand name, or entering a market, investigate whether other businesses or individuals hold relevant intellectual property rights.

    IP infringement occurs when someone uses protected intellectual property in a way that violates the rights of its owner.

    Potential issues include:

    • Choosing a business or product name that conflicts with an existing trademark
    • Developing or commercializing technology covered by another party's enforceable patent
    • Using images, text, software, or other copyrighted material without the necessary permission or licence
    • Incorporating third-party software without following its licence conditions
    • Using confidential information obtained from a previous employer, partner, or other organization without authorization
    • Creating a product design that conflicts with existing industrial design rights

    Finding a similar product or brand doesn't automatically mean you're infringing someone's rights.

    Likewise, finding no obvious match doesn't guarantee that your proposed name, product, or technology is safe to use.

    The relevant rights, jurisdictions, and circumstances matter.

    Use early research to identify potential concerns, then seek qualified IP advice when the risks or business decisions justify it.

    Understand freedom to operate

    Freedom to operate (FTO) refers to whether you can carry out specific business activities, such as making, using, or selling a product, without infringing relevant IP rights held by others.

    This is particularly important for technology businesses developing new products or processes.

    For example, you might independently develop an invention that you believe is original.

    However, another company may already hold a patent covering certain aspects of the technology in a market you intend to serve.

    Even if you obtain your own patent for an improvement, that doesn't automatically give you permission to use another party's protected invention.

    A professional freedom-to-operate assessment may involve reviewing relevant patents, claims, jurisdictions, legal status, and the activities your business intends to undertake.

    A basic online search is not the same as a legal freedom-to-operate assessment.

    At the Discovery stage, your goal is to recognize potential issues early and determine whether more detailed investigation is needed.

    Consider ownership and confidentiality

    As you work with co-founders, employees, contractors, researchers, and external partners, be clear about who owns the intellectual property being created.

    Don't assume that paying someone to develop software, create a design, or contribute to a project automatically gives your company every relevant IP right.

    Ownership can depend on the circumstances, applicable law, and the agreements in place.

    Consider:

    • Who created the work or invention?
    • Was it developed before or after the venture was established?
    • Were third-party resources, technologies, or materials involved?
    • Are there employment, contractor, university, or partnership agreements that affect ownership?
    • Does the company have the rights it needs to use and commercialize the work?

    Confidentiality is another important consideration.

    Sharing technical details, inventions, or other sensitive information publicly may affect your ability to protect certain IP rights later.

    Before publishing technical information, demonstrating an invention, or discussing sensitive details with external parties, consider whether confidentiality measures or professional advice are needed.

    Patent disclosure rules vary between countries, so timing can matter if you intend to seek protection internationally.

    Build an IP strategy that grows with your business

    You don't need a complex IP portfolio to begin thinking strategically about intellectual property.

    Start by identifying the IP relevant to your business and the decisions that may affect it.

    Your approach may include:

    • Researching existing trademarks, patents, and other relevant rights
    • Keeping records of your inventions and original work
    • Reviewing third-party software and content licences
    • Clarifying ownership with people contributing to your venture
    • Protecting confidential business information
    • Identifying inventions, names, or designs that may eventually need formal protection
    • Seeking professional guidance before making important disclosure, registration, or commercialization decisions

    Your IP strategy should evolve alongside your business.

    Some assets may become more valuable as you validate your solution and prepare to enter the market. Others may not justify the cost of formal registration.

    The goal is to make informed decisions rather than pursuing protection simply because it's available.

    Business tools

    Initial IP inventory

    An IP inventory helps you identify the intellectual property your business has created, uses, or may need to protect.

    Start by listing relevant assets and recording what you know about them.

    IP asset Type of IP Creator or owner Current status Questions or next steps
    Business name Trademark
    Product design Industrial design or other rights
    Software code Copyright and licences
    Technical invention Potential patent or trade secret
    Confidential process Potential trade secret

    These are examples. The relevant IP rights will depend on the nature of the asset.

    Include third-party materials your business relies on, but distinguish them from assets your company owns.

    Use the inventory to identify ownership questions, potential conflicts, and areas where professional advice may be valuable.

    Preliminary IP search checklist

    A preliminary IP search helps you identify existing rights that may be relevant to your business.

    Depending on your product or service, consider:

    1. Business and product names: Search for identical or similar names and trademarks in the markets you intend to serve.
    2. Technology and inventions: Investigate relevant patent publications and existing technical approaches.
    3. Product appearance: Explore registered industrial designs where visual features may be important.
    4. Software and content: Review licences and permissions for third-party code, images, written material, and other creative works.
    5. Ownership and agreements: Check whether existing contracts or relationships affect your right to use or commercialize relevant work.

    Document your search terms, sources, dates, and findings.

    Remember that preliminary searches can miss relevant rights. They do not establish legal clearance or guarantee freedom to operate.

    IP risk assessment

    Use a simple risk assessment to prioritize questions that may affect your business.

    Potential issue Business impact What you know Next step
    Similar trademark exists
    Third-party technology may be protected
    Software licence requirements are unclear
    Ownership of contractor-created work is uncertain
    Invention may be disclosed publicly

    For each issue, consider:

    • How could it affect your proposed product or business?
    • What evidence do you have?
    • What information is missing?
    • Could the issue affect an upcoming decision?
    • Should you consult a qualified IP professional before proceeding?

    Focus first on issues that could significantly affect your ability to develop, use, or commercialize your solution.

    IP strategy planning

    Use your inventory and risk assessment to outline your initial approach.

    Consider three priorities:

    1. Understand and respect existing IP

    Identify relevant third-party rights and determine where further investigation may be needed.

    2. Establish ownership and protect important information

    Clarify ownership of your work, maintain appropriate records, and consider confidentiality requirements.

    3. Plan future protection

    Identify potential trademarks, inventions, designs, or other assets that may warrant formal protection as your business develops.

    You don't need to resolve every question immediately.

    Your initial strategy should identify the decisions that matter now and the steps you'll need to take as you move toward product validation and commercialization.

    Tech support

    WIPO Global Brand Database

    The World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) provides the Global Brand Database to help users search trademark information from participating national and international collections.

    You can use it to explore existing brand names, logos, and other trademarks that may be relevant to your proposed business or product name.

    Try searching for:

    • Your proposed business name
    • Your proposed product name
    • Similar spellings or variations
    • Relevant words associated with your brand

    Review results carefully, including the jurisdictions and goods or services involved.

    The database doesn't include every possible trademark right, and a search with no matching results doesn't guarantee that a name is available to use or register.

    Canadian Trademarks Database

    The Canadian Intellectual Property Office (CIPO) provides a searchable database of Canadian trademark records.

    You can use it to investigate trademarks that may be relevant to your proposed business name, product name, or branding in Canada.

    Search for identical and similar marks, then review the available information about their status and associated goods or services.

    Trademark rights may also exist outside the registered records shown in the database.

    For important branding decisions, consider obtaining a professional trademark clearance assessment rather than relying only on your own search.

    Put it into practice

    Create an initial IP inventory and identify potential risks related to your business idea.

    Start by listing the intellectual property your venture creates, uses, or may depend on.

    Consider your business name, proposed product name, software, technical inventions, designs, written materials, and confidential information.

    Then:

    1. Identify: List the IP assets relevant to your venture, including important third-party materials.
    2. Check ownership: Record who created each asset and whether your company has the rights it needs.
    3. Research: Use the WIPO Global Brand Database and Canadian Trademarks Database to conduct preliminary searches for your proposed names.
    4. Explore risks: Identify potential trademark conflicts, third-party technology concerns, licence requirements, or ownership questions.
    5. Consider confidentiality: Review whether any inventions or sensitive information could be affected by upcoming public disclosures.
    6. Prioritize: Decide which issues need further research or professional guidance before you continue developing or commercializing your solution.

    You don't need to register anything as part of this exercise.

    The goal is to develop a clearer understanding of your IP position and identify decisions that shouldn't be overlooked.

    Before moving forward, ask yourself:

    • Do I understand which types of IP may be relevant to my business?
    • Have I investigated whether my proposed business or product name may conflict with existing rights?
    • Am I using any third-party code, content, technology, or designs that require permission or licence compliance?
    • Do I know who owns the work being created for my venture?
    • Could an upcoming presentation, demonstration, or publication affect future protection?
    • Are there potential freedom-to-operate concerns that need professional assessment?
    • Which assets might be worth protecting as the business develops?

    Summarize your findings and identify the questions you need to resolve.

    Consider reviewing your initial IP strategy with your TechAlliance advisor and seeking advice from a qualified IP lawyer, patent agent, or trademark agent where appropriate.

    Remember: The purpose of this exercise is to identify and manage potential IP risks, not to provide legal clearance.

    As you move toward the Validation stage, revisit your IP strategy before making important decisions about product development, public disclosure, branding, or commercialization.
