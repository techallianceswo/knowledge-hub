    Go-to-market planning draft

    Plan how your business will reach its first customers

    You've researched your market, explored your competition, identified potential customer segments, and spoken directly with people experiencing the problem you want to solve.

    Now it's time to start thinking about how your business could reach those customers.

    A go-to-market (GTM) strategy is a plan for introducing a product or service to a target market, reaching potential customers, communicating its value, and generating sales.

    It connects what you're building with the people who may benefit from it.

    Your go-to-market strategy can help you answer questions such as:

    • Who should we focus on reaching first?
    • Where and how can we connect with those customers?
    • What problem should our messaging emphasize?
    • How will customers learn about, evaluate, and purchase our solution?
    • Which sales, marketing, and partnership activities are worth exploring?
    • What resources will we need?
    • How will we measure whether our approach is working?

    At the Discovery stage, you're creating an initial GTM draft, not a final launch plan.

    Your understanding of the market has improved, but you may not have validated your proposed solution or demonstrated that customers are willing to pay for it.

    The purpose of this exercise is to organize your assumptions, identify promising approaches, and prepare for the experiments you'll conduct as your business develops.

    A good GTM draft gives you direction without committing you to a strategy that hasn't been tested.

    Build your plan around your customer research

    Your GTM strategy should begin with what you've learned about your potential customers.

    Review the findings from your:

    • Competition analysis
    • Potential customer segments research
    • Customer discovery interviews
    • Subject-matter expert interviews
    • Product roadmap

    Together, these resources can help you understand who your customers might be, what matters to them, and how they currently discover and evaluate solutions.

    For example, if you're developing software for small manufacturers, your research might suggest that potential customers rely on industry associations, professional referrals, or trade events when evaluating new technology.

    Those insights could make partnerships and direct outreach more promising starting points than broad consumer advertising.

    However, these are still assumptions until you've tested them.

    Prioritize channels based on evidence about customer behaviour, not simply because they're popular.

    Identify your initial target market

    Trying to reach everyone at once can make your marketing and sales efforts expensive and difficult to manage.

    Start by choosing a specific customer segment.

    Consider:

    • Who experiences the problem most frequently or intensely?
    • Who is already investing time or money in addressing it?
    • Which customers can you realistically reach?
    • Who is involved in the purchasing decision?
    • Where are those customers located?
    • Are there industry, geographic, or regulatory factors that affect your approach?

    For B2B businesses, distinguish between the person who uses the solution and the person who approves or pays for it.

    For B2C businesses, consider the customer's needs, purchasing habits, and the situations that might motivate them to act.

    Your initial target market doesn't need to be your only market forever.

    It should give you a focused starting point for learning what works.

    Understand how customers discover and buy solutions

    A customer rarely moves directly from hearing about a business to making a purchase.

    They may first recognize a problem, search for information, compare alternatives, speak with colleagues, request a demonstration, or seek approval from someone else.

    This process is often called the customer journey or buying journey.

    Understanding it can help you identify the activities and channels that deserve attention.

    Consider the following stages:

    Awareness

    How do potential customers recognize the problem and discover possible solutions?

    Examples might include online searches, professional networks, social media, referrals, and industry events.

    Consideration

    How do customers compare different approaches?

    They may review websites, read case studies, ask peers for recommendations, or speak with vendors.

    Decision

    What needs to happen before they commit?

    Depending on the business, this could involve a purchase, trial, demonstration, proposal, procurement review, or internal approval.

    Onboarding and retention

    What will customers need after they begin using the solution?

    Think about support, education, implementation, and the experience required to help them receive value.

    You may not know every step yet.

    Use your customer interviews to identify what you've learned and what still needs to be investigated.

    Explore distribution and acquisition channels

    A distribution channel is how your product or service reaches customers.

    A customer acquisition channel is how you attract and convert potential customers.

    Sometimes these overlap, but they aren't always the same.

    For example, a software company might attract customers through LinkedIn but deliver its product through its own website.

    Potential channels include:

    Channel How it might be used
    Direct sales Reach potential customers through personalized conversations and outreach
    Website and search Help customers discover and evaluate your offering online
    Social media Build awareness, share useful information, and connect with relevant communities
    Email marketing Maintain relationships with people who have agreed to receive communications
    Industry events Meet potential customers, partners, and industry professionals
    Referral networks Reach new customers through trusted introductions
    Strategic partnerships Work with organizations that already serve your target audience
    Marketplaces and platforms Make products or services available through established platforms
    Retail or distributors Reach customers through physical stores, resellers, or distribution partners
    Paid advertising Test targeted messages and reach potential customers through advertising platforms

    Not every channel will make sense for your venture.

    Consider where your target customers spend their time, how they make purchasing decisions, and what it would cost to reach them.

    At this stage, selecting one or two promising channels to investigate is often more useful than trying to establish a presence everywhere.

    Plan your sales and outreach approach

    Your GTM draft should explain how you expect to start conversations with potential customers.

    Depending on your business, this might involve:

    • Founder-led outreach to prospective customers
    • Introductions through professional networks
    • Relationships with industry associations
    • Participation in relevant events
    • Partnerships with complementary businesses
    • Inbound inquiries through your website
    • Targeted email or social outreach

    For early-stage ventures, founder-led sales conversations can be especially useful because they provide direct feedback about customer needs, objections, and buying decisions.

    Before investing in a large sales team or complex systems, learn whether your outreach approach generates meaningful conversations.

    Consider:

    • Who should you contact?
    • Why would they be interested in speaking with you?
    • What value can you offer during the conversation?
    • What action are you asking them to take?
    • How will you track responses and follow-ups?

    Remember that contacting potential customers for research is different from selling to them.

    Be transparent about the purpose of your outreach and follow applicable privacy and anti-spam requirements.

    Develop your initial marketing message

    Your marketing message should connect the customer problem to the value your proposed solution aims to provide.

    Start with what you learned during customer discovery.

    Consider:

    • What problem does the customer recognize?
    • How do customers describe that problem in their own words?
    • Why does solving it matter?
    • What outcome would be valuable to them?
    • How might your proposed approach differ from existing alternatives?

    Avoid making claims about results, capabilities, or customer satisfaction that you haven't demonstrated.

    At the Discovery stage, your message is a hypothesis to test.

    For example:

    Problem-focused message:

    "Preparing weekly reports shouldn't require hours of manual data collection."

    Proposed value message:

    "We're exploring a simpler way for small teams to bring their reporting information together."

    The second example communicates a direction without claiming that the product already delivers a proven result.

    As you validate your solution, you can make your messaging more specific and evidence-based.

    Keep your first GTM plan capital-efficient

    Early-stage ventures often have limited time, money, and people available.

    Your initial GTM approach should help you learn without requiring a large upfront investment.

    Before committing resources, ask:

    • Can we test this channel on a small scale?
    • Can the founders manage the initial outreach?
    • What do we need to prepare before testing?
    • How much time and money would the test require?
    • What would indicate that the approach is promising?
    • What would cause us to change direction?

    A small number of focused experiments can provide more useful information than a broad campaign with unclear objectives.

    For example, you might compare personalized outreach to a small group of potential customers with introductions through an industry association.

    The goal is to learn which approach generates relevant conversations, not simply which produces the most impressions or clicks.

    Business tools

    Customer Zoom Tool

    The Customer Zoom Tool, developed by Moves the Needle, helps you identify specific potential customers rather than relying only on broad market segments or general customer personas.

    Instead of starting with categories such as age, industry, or company size, the tool encourages you to focus on the traits and observable behaviours that distinguish people who experience the problem you're trying to solve.

    Using insights from your customer discovery interviews, work through three steps:

    1. Identify distinctive traits: Think about one person you've interviewed and list at least 10 characteristics that make them relevant to your proposed solution. Select the three most distinctive traits.
    2. Identify observable behaviours: List at least 10 actions or habits that could help you recognize someone experiencing the problem. Select the three most distinctive behaviours.
    3. Describe a specific customer: Use those traits and behaviours to describe a real person who represents the type of customer you want to reach.

    Repeat the exercise with other interview participants to explore whether different customer groups share similar problems, traits, and behaviours.

    If your business serves multiple customer groups, such as buyers and end users, consider completing a separate exercise for each.

    How does this help your GTM strategy?

    A more specific understanding of your customer can help you decide:

    • Where to find people who match your target customer profile
    • Which communities, networks, or organizations they participate in
    • What behaviours might indicate they're experiencing the problem
    • How to make your outreach and messaging more relevant
    • Which customer groups to prioritize during your initial GTM experiments

    Remember that the tool should be grounded in real customer interviews, not an imaginary ideal customer.

    The goal isn't to create a perfect persona. It's to identify recognizable people whose needs and behaviours can guide your next steps.

    Go-to-market planning canvas

    Use a simple planning canvas to bring the main elements of your strategy together.

    GTM element Questions to answer
    Target customer Who are we trying to reach first?
    Customer problem What important problem are we addressing?
    Proposed value What outcome do we hope to help customers achieve?
    Competition What alternatives are customers using today?
    Positioning Why might customers consider our approach?
    Distribution How will customers access our product or service?
    Acquisition channels How will potential customers discover us?
    Sales approach How will we start and manage customer conversations?
    Marketing activities What content, messages, or materials might we need?
    Partnerships Which organizations could help us reach customers?
    Resources What people, tools, time, and budget are available?
    Metrics How will we evaluate our initial experiments?
    Assumptions What still needs to be tested?

    You don't need a perfect answer for every category.

    The canvas should help you see which decisions are supported by research and which require further investigation.

    Channel prioritization matrix

    Compare potential acquisition and distribution channels before deciding where to focus.

    Channel Customer relevance Cost and effort Evidence Next experiment
    Direct outreach
    Industry partnerships
    Search and website
    Social media
    Events

    For each channel, consider:

    • Do our target customers actually use this channel?
    • Can we reach the right people through it?
    • What would it cost to test?
    • What resources or partners would we need?
    • What evidence supports our assumptions?
    • What would we learn from a small experiment?

    Select one or two channels for your initial testing plan.

    Avoid treating a channel as proven simply because competitors use it.

    Six-week GTM planning framework

    A six-week plan can help you organize your preparation and early experiments into manageable activities.

    At the Discovery stage, treat this as a planning baseline. Activities that involve selling or promoting an unvalidated solution may need to wait until the Validation stage.

    Week Focus Suggested activities
    1 Review research Consolidate customer insights, competition findings, and assumptions
    2 Define your approach Select an initial segment, draft positioning, and identify promising channels
    3 Prepare materials Develop outreach messages, a basic value proposition, and any materials needed for research
    4 Test channel assumptions Explore selected channels through conversations, introductions, or small-scale experiments
    5 Review early signals Compare responses, objections, customer relevance, and effort required
    6 Refine the plan Update priorities, identify remaining assumptions, and prepare the next round of validation

    Your plan should reflect your actual resources and readiness.

    You may need more time to investigate a market, secure introductions, or validate your proposed solution before conducting commercial experiments.

    GTM metrics and learning tracker

    Metrics help you evaluate whether your activities are producing useful results.

    For early-stage businesses, focus on a manageable number of indicators.

    Potential metrics include:

    • Outreach response rate: The percentage of contacted prospects who respond.
    • Qualified conversations: The number of conversations with people who match your target customer profile.
    • Meeting conversion rate: The percentage of relevant outreach attempts that result in meetings.
    • Channel cost: The time and money required to test a particular channel.
    • Website conversion rate: The percentage of visitors who complete a defined action, when a website experiment is relevant.
    • Customer acquisition cost (CAC): The cost of acquiring a paying customer, once you have enough actual acquisition data to calculate it meaningfully.
    • Sales cycle length: The time between an initial sales opportunity and a completed purchase, once sales activity begins.
    • Customer feedback: The recurring questions, concerns, and objections you hear during conversations.

    Not all of these metrics will be relevant or measurable during Discovery.

    Start by tracking the activities you can actually perform and the signals that help you make decisions.

    Be cautious about applying general industry benchmarks. Results can vary significantly by market, channel, product, price, and customer segment.

    Where reliable benchmarks are unavailable, establish your own baseline through initial experiments.

    Tech support

    ChatGPT / Claude / DeepSeek / Augure[1]

    They can help you organize market research, compare potential GTM channels, draft outreach messages, and create an initial project plan.

    You can also use them to challenge assumptions and identify questions that need further research.

    AI-generated recommendations should be treated as hypotheses until they're supported by credible sources or real customer evidence.

COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

[INSERT SUMMARY FROM COMPETITION ANALYSIS]

POTENTIAL CUSTOMER SEGMENTS

[INSERT SUMMARY FROM CUSTOMER SEGMENT RESEARCH]

CUSTOMER DISCOVERY FINDINGS

[INSERT SUMMARY OF INTERVIEWS, CUSTOMER PROBLEMS, CURRENT ALTERNATIVES, AND BUYING BEHAVIOURS]

SUBJECT-MATTER EXPERT INSIGHTS

[INSERT RELEVANT FINDINGS, IF AVAILABLE]

PRODUCT ROADMAP

[INSERT INITIAL PRIORITIES AND DESIRED CUSTOMER OUTCOMES]

YOUR TASK

Research relevant good practices, market evidence, and customer acquisition approaches to develop an initial six-week GTM plan.

Prioritize recommendations that are realistic for our development stage, target market, team capacity, and available budget.

Address the following areas:

1. DISTRIBUTION AND ACQUISITION CHANNELS

- Identify the most promising distribution and customer acquisition channels.
- Explain why each channel may fit our customer segment and buying behaviour.
- Distinguish how customers discover the offering from how they access or purchase it.
- Identify the preparation, resources, and potential partners required.
- Compare expected cost, effort, and uncertainty.
- Recommend one or two channels to investigate first.

2. SALES AND BUSINESS DEVELOPMENT

- Recommend a capital-efficient approach to initial outreach.
- Evaluate founder-led sales, events, industry associations, partnerships, referrals, and other relevant approaches.
- Explain whether hiring sales development representatives (SDRs) would be justified at our current stage.
- Suggest simple systems for tracking contacts, conversations, follow-ups, and outcomes.
- Identify any important considerations for outreach compliance and privacy.

3. MARKETING AND PROMOTION

- Recommend the essential marketing materials and activities needed at our current stage.
- Evaluate the relevance of website content, search, social media, email, events, partnerships, and paid advertising.
- Identify which activities should be tested first, deferred, or avoided.
- Suggest messages based on customer problems and desired outcomes.
- Do not invent customer testimonials, proven results, traction, or product capabilities.

4. METRICS AND BENCHMARKS

- Recommend a small set of useful KPIs for our stage.
- Define each metric and explain how to calculate it.
- Identify credible benchmarks where available and relevant.
- Cite the source, date, geography, and business context of each benchmark.
- Do not invent benchmarks or imply that industry averages are guaranteed targets.
- If reliable benchmarks are unavailable, recommend how to establish our own baseline.

5. SIX-WEEK PROJECT PLAN

Create one consolidated timeline covering all recommended activities.

For each task, include:
- Week
- Objective
- Activity
- Suggested owner or role
- Dependencies
- Expected deliverable
- Estimated effort or cost, where supportable
- Success or learning indicator

Identify key milestones and decision points.

Separate activities appropriate for Discovery from commercial activities that should wait until the solution is sufficiently validated.

RESEARCH REQUIREMENTS

Use credible and relevant sources where available, including:
- Government and statistical sources such as Statistics Canada
- Industry research and reports
- Relevant business and startup publications
- Competitor websites and publicly available materials
- Customer reviews and community discussions
- Search and competitive intelligence tools

Provide links for important factual claims, market observations, and benchmarks.

Clearly distinguish:
- Verified facts
- Findings provided in the venture information
- Reasonable assumptions
- Recommendations that require testing

Do not invent market data, competitor performance, customer behaviour, costs, conversion rates, or source citations.

If you cannot access live internet research, say so explicitly and do not present unsupported claims as verified research.

If important information is missing, state the assumptions used and explain how they affect the recommendations.

FINAL OUTPUT

Provide:

A. Executive summary of the proposed GTM direction
B. Prioritized distribution and acquisition channels
C. Sales and business development approach
D. Marketing and promotion priorities
E. KPI definitions and supported benchmarks
F. One consolidated six-week project plan
G. Key assumptions, risks, and questions to validate
H. Sources and references

Keep the recommendations practical, specific, and proportionate to the venture's stage and resources.

    Review the output carefully.

    Check the cited sources, question unsupported assumptions, and adjust the recommendations to reflect what you've learned from real customers.

    Don't treat the AI-generated plan as a final strategy or a substitute for testing your approach.

    Put it into practice

    Create a first draft of your go-to-market strategy.

    Your goal isn't to launch a full marketing and sales campaign. It's to develop a focused plan that you can test and improve as your solution becomes ready.

    Start by gathering the research you've completed during the Idea and Discovery stages.

    Then:

    1. Choose your initial customer segment. Review your customer discovery interviews and complete the Customer Zoom Tool to identify the distinctive traits and behaviours of the people you want to reach first. Use those findings to refine your target customer profile.
    2. Define the customer problem. Summarize the challenge you're addressing and the outcome customers want.
    3. Draft your value proposition. Explain the value your proposed solution aims to provide without making unverified claims.
    4. Identify possible channels. List the ways you might reach and serve your target customers.
    5. Prioritize. Select one or two channels based on customer relevance, available resources, and supporting evidence.
    6. Plan outreach and promotion. Identify the conversations, materials, partnerships, or experiments needed to investigate those channels.
    7. Choose metrics. Decide what information will help you evaluate your approach.
    8. Build a six-week plan. Organize your tasks, responsibilities, dependencies, and milestones into one timeline.

    You can use the suggested AI prompt to help research your options and develop the initial plan.

    Before considering your draft complete, ask yourself:

    • Can I explain why I've chosen this customer segment?
    • Do I have evidence that my target customers use the channels I'm considering?
    • Does my messaging reflect problems identified during customer discovery?
    • Am I clear about how customers might discover, evaluate, and purchase the solution?
    • Have I considered partnerships and referrals as well as direct marketing?
    • Is my plan realistic for my available team, time, and budget?
    • Have I separated verified findings from assumptions?
    • What can I test now, and what should wait until the solution is validated?
    • What would cause me to change my approach?

    Your GTM draft should be a working document.

    Review it as you test your solution, learn more about your customers, and gather evidence about which channels and messages are effective.

    Consider discussing your draft with your TechAlliance advisor to review your assumptions, priorities, and proposed experiments.

    The goal of Discovery is to develop an informed direction. The next stage is about testing whether your proposed solution and approach can deliver value to real customers.
