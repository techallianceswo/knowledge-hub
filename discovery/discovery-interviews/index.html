    Customer discovery

    Understand your customers before building your solution

    You've researched your market, explored potential customer segments, and identified a problem your business might solve.

    Now it's time to find out whether that problem exists in the lives of real people.

    Customer discovery is the process of learning directly from potential customers about their experiences, challenges, behaviours, and needs.

    It often involves one-on-one interviews where you ask people to describe how they handle a particular situation, what difficulties they experience, and what they've already done to address them.

    The goal is to move beyond assumptions and understand the problem from the customer's perspective.

    At this stage, you're not trying to convince people to buy your product or approve your idea. You're trying to learn whether the problem is real, who experiences it, and how important it is to them.

    Customer discovery is about validating the problem, not selling the solution.

    Why customer discovery matters

    Market research can help you identify trends, potential customer groups, and opportunities. But it doesn't always explain how people experience a problem in their daily lives.

    Speaking directly with potential customers can help you understand:

    • The problem: What challenges do people actually experience?
    • The context: When, where, and why does the problem occur?
    • The impact: How much time, money, effort, or frustration does it create?
    • Current alternatives: How are people addressing the problem today?
    • Customer priorities: Is solving the problem important enough for them to take action?
    • Customer segments: Which groups appear to experience the problem most frequently or intensely?

    You may discover that your original assumptions were correct, partially correct, or completely different from what customers experience.

    All of these outcomes provide useful information.

    Learning that a problem isn't important to your intended customers can help you reconsider your approach before investing significant time and resources into building a solution.

    Talk about experiences, not hypothetical situations

    One of the most important principles of customer discovery is to focus on what people have actually experienced.

    For example, asking someone, "Would you use an app that makes scheduling easier?" may lead to an encouraging answer.

    But that answer doesn't tell you whether scheduling is a significant problem, how often it occurs, or whether the person would change their current approach.

    Instead, ask questions such as:

    • "Can you tell me about the last time you had difficulty scheduling something?"
    • "What happened?"
    • "How did you handle it?"
    • "What made that situation difficult?"
    • "Have you tried anything to make the process easier?"

    These questions encourage people to share specific experiences rather than speculate about what they might do in the future.

    What customers have done is generally more useful evidence than what they say they might do.

    Who should you interview?

    Start with people who match the potential customer segments you've identified.

    For example, if your proposed solution addresses a problem experienced by restaurant managers, prioritize conversations with people who manage restaurants or regularly perform the relevant work.

    Avoid relying only on friends, family members, or people who already support your idea. Their feedback may be influenced by their relationship with you.

    Consider reaching potential interview participants through:

    • Professional networks and industry associations
    • LinkedIn and relevant online communities
    • Local business and community events
    • Introductions from existing contacts
    • Organizations serving your target customer segment

    When reaching out, explain that you're researching a problem and would value their experience.

    Make it clear that you're not contacting them to sell something.

    For B2B businesses, remember that the person experiencing the problem may not be the person responsible for purchasing a solution.

    You may eventually need to understand the perspectives of users, managers, and decision-makers.

    How to conduct a customer discovery interview

    You don't need a complicated process to get started. A focused conversation with a clear learning objective can provide valuable insights.

    1. Define what you want to learn

    Before the interview, identify the assumptions you're trying to investigate.

    For example:

    "We believe independent retailers regularly struggle with managing inventory across multiple sales channels."

    Your interview should help you understand whether that challenge exists, how it affects retailers, and what they're currently doing about it.

    2. Prepare open-ended questions

    Create a short interview guide focused on the customer's experiences.

    Use it to keep the conversation on track, but don't treat it as a script you must follow exactly.

    Be prepared to ask follow-up questions when someone shares an interesting experience.

    3. Explain the purpose of the conversation

    Introduce yourself and explain that you're learning about how people experience a particular challenge.

    Let the participant know there are no right or wrong answers.

    If you'd like to record the conversation, ask for permission first and explain how the recording will be used and stored.

    4. Listen more than you speak

    Give the participant time to explain their experiences.

    Avoid interrupting, suggesting answers, or immediately introducing your proposed solution.

    When they describe a problem, ask for a specific example.

    Questions such as "What happened next?" or "Can you tell me more about that?" can help you understand the situation in greater detail.

    5. Explore the impact and current alternatives

    Try to understand what the problem costs the customer in time, money, effort, or missed opportunities.

    Ask what they've already tried and why their current approach may or may not be working.

    6. Close and document what you learned

    Before ending, ask whether there's anything important you haven't covered.

    You can also ask whether the participant knows someone else who might be willing to share their experience.

    After the conversation, review your notes while the details are still fresh.

    Record what the person actually said or described, and distinguish those observations from your own interpretations.

    Business tools

    Customer discovery interview guide

    Use the following questions as a starting point for your interviews.

    Adapt them to the problem you're investigating and the customer segment you're speaking with.

    Understanding the customer

    • Can you tell me a little about your role or daily activities?
    • How do you currently handle [RELEVANT ACTIVITY OR PROCESS]?
    • What tools or methods do you use?

    Exploring the problem

    • What's the most challenging part of that process?
    • Can you tell me about the last time you experienced that challenge?
    • What happened?
    • How often does something like that occur?
    • What makes it difficult?

    Understanding the impact

    • How does this challenge affect your work or daily life?
    • How much time or effort does it typically require?
    • What happens when the problem isn't resolved?
    • Who else is affected?

    Exploring current alternatives

    • What have you tried to address the problem?
    • How are you handling it today?
    • What works well about your current approach?
    • What doesn't work as well?
    • Have you spent money or resources trying to improve the situation?

    Closing the conversation

    • Is there anything important about this experience that I haven't asked?
    • Is there someone else you think I should speak with?
    • Would you be comfortable if I contacted you with additional questions?

    You don't need to ask every question in every interview.

    Focus on understanding the participant's experiences and follow up when their answers reveal something important.

    Customer discovery notes template

    Use a consistent format to document what you learn from each conversation.

    Category Notes
    Interview date
    Customer segment
    Participant role or context
    Main problem described
    Specific example shared
    Frequency of the problem
    Impact on the customer
    Current solution or workaround
    Previous attempts to solve it
    Important observations
    Assumptions supported or challenged
    Questions to investigate further

    Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information.

    If you record or transcribe interviews, obtain appropriate consent and store the information securely.

    Problem validation matrix

    After conducting several interviews, compare your findings to identify patterns.

    Create a table that summarizes the problems mentioned by different participants.

    Problem Participants who mentioned it Reported impact Current alternatives Evidence or examples
    Problem A
    Problem B
    Problem C

    Look for:

    • Problems mentioned repeatedly
    • Similar experiences across participants
    • Evidence of time, money, or effort already spent
    • Workarounds people have developed
    • Differences between customer segments
    • Findings that challenge your original assumptions

    Don't assume a problem is important simply because several people mention it.

    Consider its frequency, severity, consequences, and whether customers are already taking action to address it.

    Interview synthesis

    Once you've collected several interviews, summarize what you've learned.

    Try organizing your findings into four categories:

    1. Customer problems: What difficulties are people experiencing?
    2. Current behaviours: How are they addressing those difficulties?
    3. Problem impact: What consequences do those difficulties create?
    4. Unanswered questions: What do you still need to investigate?

    Use the summary to refine your understanding of the customer segment and the problem.

    If the findings are inconsistent, you may need to speak with more people or reconsider whether you're interviewing the right customer group.

    Tech support

    Google Forms

    Google Forms can help you collect basic information from potential interview participants before scheduling a conversation.

    For example, you can use a short form to confirm whether someone fits the customer segment you're researching.

    Forms can also help organize contact preferences and scheduling information.

    Keep the form brief. A questionnaire shouldn't replace the deeper conversations needed for customer discovery.

    Calendly

    Calendly is a scheduling platform that can help you arrange interviews without exchanging multiple emails.

    You can share your availability and allow participants to choose a suitable meeting time.

    This can be useful when coordinating interviews with people outside your organization.

    Google Meet

    Google Meet is a video meeting platform you can use to conduct remote customer discovery interviews.

    Remote conversations can help you reach potential customers outside your immediate geographic area.

    If you plan to record a meeting, confirm that the feature is available in your account and obtain the participant's permission.

    Google Sheets

    Google Sheets can help you organize interview notes, compare findings, and identify recurring themes.

    Create a spreadsheet using the customer discovery notes template or problem validation matrix.

    Keep interview records organized and limit access to people who need the information.

    ChatGPT / Claude / DeepSeek / Augure[1]

    These can help you review an interview guide for leading questions, organize anonymized notes, and identify potential themes across interviews.

    They can also help you prepare neutral follow-up questions.

    However, AI-generated summaries may overlook important details or misinterpret what participants said.

    Review the original notes or transcripts before drawing conclusions.

    AI-generated customer personas or simulated interviews are not substitutes for conversations with real potential customers.

    Avoid sharing identifiable participant information, confidential business details, or interview recordings with AI tools without appropriate consent and safeguards.

    [1]: Augure is the only fully Canadian option from this list

    Put it into practice

    Conduct your first round of customer discovery interviews.

    Start by selecting one potential customer segment and one problem you want to investigate.

    Write down your current assumptions:

    • Who experiences the problem?
    • When does the problem occur?
    • Why do you believe it's important?
    • How do you think customers currently address it?

    Next, prepare a short interview guide using the questions provided on this page.

    Aim to speak with at least five people who fit your target customer segment as an initial round of discovery.

    This is a starting point, not a fixed requirement for validating a problem. You may need additional interviews as you learn more.

    For each conversation:

    1. Prepare: Confirm that the participant fits your target segment and identify what you want to learn.
    2. Listen: Ask about real experiences and avoid pitching your proposed solution.
    3. Document: Record the problems, behaviours, workarounds, and consequences described.
    4. Compare: Look for similarities and differences across interviews.
    5. Reflect: Identify which assumptions were supported, challenged, or left unanswered.

    Once you've completed your initial interviews, summarize your findings.

    Ask yourself:

    • Did participants describe the problem without needing me to suggest it?
    • Can they recall specific situations when it occurred?
    • How frequently or seriously does the problem affect them?
    • Are they already spending time, money, or effort addressing it?
    • Did similar patterns appear across multiple interviews?
    • What surprised me or contradicted my assumptions?
    • Do I need to investigate a different customer segment or problem?

    Don't worry if your findings challenge your original idea.

    The purpose of customer discovery is to learn what matters to potential customers, not to prove that your first assumption was correct.

    Consider reviewing your interview findings with your TechAlliance advisor to decide what to investigate next.

    As you build confidence that a meaningful problem exists, you'll be better prepared to explore and eventually validate a solution.
